<Lens level={1} title="Who holds the truth?">

Every system answers one question: **who decides what is true, and who can change it?**

A centralised platform has an operator. That operator can recover an account, freeze a transaction and deploy a fix quickly — and can also censor, lose data or become a single point of compromise. A decentralised system distributes control, but coordination gets slower, governance becomes political, and new failure modes appear.

Neither is “more secure” without a threat model. A hospital records system and a public cryptocurrency have different adversaries, availability needs and trust assumptions.

## Decentralisation is a spectrum

1. **Centralised:** one database and one administrator.
2. **Federated / hybrid:** several operators coordinate; data or control is partitioned.
3. **Consortium:** a known validator set shares authority.
4. **Permissionless peer-to-peer:** open participation and protocol-enforced consensus.

## What trust still means

Decentralisation distributes trust; it does not delete it. Clients still trust protocol code, cryptographic assumptions, wallet software, governance processes and honest coalitions. <Cite id="sp800-207" locator="§2" />

## Three concrete systems

- **Online banking:** one bank controls the ledger, identity, fraud rules and recovery. Customers accept that trust because the operator is regulated, auditable and legally accountable. A breach or insider abuse is catastrophic.
- **Healthcare consortium:** several hospitals share a research model or audit trail. No single hospital should see another's raw records, so the protocol coordinates computation while keeping data local.
- **Public blockchain:** anyone can validate the chain, but finality depends on a majority of hash power or stake. The absence of a central operator removes some censorship risks and creates governance, energy and scalability costs.

The security question is not “centralised or decentralised?” but “which trust assumptions does this application require, and what happens when each assumption fails?”

## A trust worksheet

For each component, ask:

- What can it read?
- What can it write or delete?
- Can it change the rules or upgrade the protocol?
- What happens if it is unavailable?
- What happens if it is malicious?
- Who can recover a lost credential?

If the answer is “the operator” for every row, you have a centralised system with a single trust boundary. If the answers differ, document the trust set and the quorum required to change state. That worksheet is more useful than a decentralisation score.

### Build the threat model before choosing the topology

Consider a regional payments platform that must settle transfers, prevent double spending and remain available during a national network incident. Its security objectives are ledger integrity, authorisation, non-repudiation, recovery and bounded settlement delay. The passive attacker observes traffic; the active attacker replays messages; the insider attempts an unauthorised transfer; the compromised operator tries to rewrite history. A regulator and a customer also need evidence that the platform behaved correctly. These are different adversaries with different costs, so counting validators alone cannot establish security.

A central operator has strong incentives to protect the ledger because fraud and outage damage its business, but it can also censor or disclose records. Validators may favour their own members or collude with a counterparty. In a permissionless network, an attacker buys hash power or stake; measurable cost does not guarantee honest participation. The defender preserves protocol credibility, while attackers seek short-term extraction or disruption.

Three misconceptions cause bad architecture decisions. First, **more replicas do not mean more independence**: seven validators operated by one cloud account have one effective failure domain. Second, **consensus does not establish truth outside the protocol**: if every validator receives the same false exchange rate, they can agree on a false ledger entry. Third, **decentralisation does not remove recovery**: keys, governance clients and human decisions still concentrate authority. Measure the smallest coalition that can rewrite state, censor, recover or upgrade; that is the real trust boundary.

| Central server | One operator | Low latency, simple recovery, strong consistency | Censorship, single point of failure, concentrated breach |

Consensus orders events. Crash-fault-tolerant protocols tolerate crashes; Byzantine fault-tolerant protocols also tolerate arbitrary behaviour. <Cite id="castro-liskov1999" locator="pp. 173–186" /> With oral messages, Byzantine agreement requires more than two-thirds of participants to be honest. <Cite id="lamport1982" locator="pp. 382–401" /> Proof-of-work replaces identity with computational cost and probabilistic finality. <Cite id="nakamoto2008" locator="§3–5" />

Recovery differs sharply. Centralised systems use operator workflows. Consortiums use policy. Permissionless systems bind identity to keys, so losing a key often means losing the asset; multi-signature and social recovery move the trust rather than remove it.

Do not grant implicit trust based on network location. Authenticate and authorise every request. <Cite id="sp800-207" locator="§3" /> This applies to centralised and decentralised designs alike.
</Callout>



## A walk through the architecture choices

**Central server.** Clients authenticate to one authority. Replication and backups provide availability; a control-plane compromise affects everyone. Recovery is a support workflow.

**Federated / hybrid.** Participants coordinate through protocols. The hard parts are identity, policy consistency, data minimisation and dispute resolution. Federated learning is a data-plane example: local data, shared updates, central or delegated aggregation.

**Consortium.** A known validator set signs blocks or decisions. Membership is permissioned; governance decides who joins, who leaves and how upgrades happen. A majority or supermajority of validators can change state, so validator capture is the key risk.

**Permissionless peer-to-peer.** Open participation and protocol-enforced rules. Security depends on the consensus mechanism, client diversity, network assumptions and the cost of acquiring influence. Finality may be probabilistic, and upgrades may require social coordination.

## Recovery and identity

In a centralised system, account recovery is an operator workflow with identity proofing. In a consortium, it is a policy decision with quorum approval. In a permissionless system, identity is a key pair; recovery is multi-signature, social recovery or loss. Each answer changes the threat model: recovery mechanisms are often the weakest link because they bypass the cryptography.

### A concrete consortium walkthrough

Suppose seven hospitals operate a shared audit ledger. Four organisations control one validator each, an independent infrastructure provider controls two, and a patient representative controls one. The charter allows at most two Byzantine validators and requires five signatures to commit a batch. Membership changes require five of seven approvals plus a thirty-day notice period. Configuration choices include a three-second block interval, a ten-second view timeout, a maximum batch size and a rule for handling an offline validator. These numbers are not universally correct: they express the desired recovery time, the observed network delay and the tolerated denial-of-service window.

A transfer begins at a hospital gateway. The gateway authenticates the user, assigns a monotonic request identifier and signs the transaction. A validator checks the signature, account balance, replay identifier and clinical policy before pre-preparing the request. Other validators validate independently rather than trusting the proposer's assertion. They prepare only when the transaction is valid, then commit after collecting a certificate containing five matching votes. The ledger appends the transaction and returns a receipt. A restarting validator requests a checkpoint or replays certified entries rather than inferring history from its block.

The protocol must distinguish ordinary latency from a partition. If five validators remain connected, the system continues to commit. If a network split leaves three and four validators, neither side has five votes, so both stop committing new transfers. Reads may continue from local checkpoints with a clear freshness warning, but the payment endpoint must reject requests that require settlement certainty. Once the partition heals, validators exchange certificates and converge on the highest certified sequence number. Fencing tokens prevent an isolated site administrator from acting on stale authority.

### Configuration choices that change the security model

Membership policy is as important as the consensus code. If the same person can add validators and approve the resulting policy change, decentralisation is nominal. Limit emergency membership changes to a documented threshold, separate proposer authority from admission authority and test removal of a malicious validator. Rate limits protect against spam, but overly broad limits can become censorship; quotas should bind to authenticated principals.

Under a network partition, a distributed store cannot guarantee both linearizable consistency and availability for every request.

Partitions are not optional; the design must choose behaviour. <Cite id="gilbert-lynch2002" locator="Vol. 33, No. 2, pp. 51–59" />

For deterministic oral-message Byzantine agreement with $n$ parties and at most $f$ faults, $n \ge 3f + 1$ is necessary and sufficient. PBFT makes this practical for small known validator sets. <Cite id="lamport1982" locator="pp. 382–401" /> <Cite id="castro-liskov1999" locator="pp. 173–186" /> Proof-of-work changes the assumption to an honest majority of hash power and introduces mining concentration, which has been measured in practice. <Cite id="gencer2018" locator="pp. 439–457" />

A “decentralised” protocol may still trust a foundation, core developers, a bridge, an oracle, a wallet vendor and a cloud provider. Decentralisation claims should name those components and the quorum needed to change the protocol.
</Callout>

Federated systems are a deliberate middle ground: they distribute data or authority while retaining coordination points. They can reduce concentration without pretending coordination is free. <Cite id="kairouz2021" locator="Vol. 14, pp. 1–210" />



## CAP in practice

Under a partition, a system must decide whether to reject requests (favour consistency) or serve potentially stale data (favour availability). The theorem is not a design recipe; it forces an explicit choice per operation. A payment ledger and a social feed can choose differently.

Consistency also has levels: linearizability, sequential consistency, causal consistency and eventual consistency. A security review should ask what a client can observe during a partition and whether that observation enables fraud, replay or privilege confusion.

## Byzantine quorum arithmetic

With $n$ replicas and at most $f$ Byzantine faults, safety and liveness require $n \ge 3f + 1$ under classical oral-message assumptions. Quorums of size $2f+1$ intersect in at least $f+1$ replicas, so at least one honest replica appears in both. This is why a malicious minority cannot convince two honest clients of conflicting histories. <Cite id="lamport1982" locator="pp. 382–401" />

Proof-of-work replaces the quorum with an economic majority. Its finality is probabilistic and its security depends on assumptions about mining concentration and network propagation. Measurement studies show why “decentralised” is a spectrum. <Cite id="gencer2018" locator="pp. 439–457" />

Write down who can read state, who can write state, how many faults are tolerated, what synchrony is assumed and how membership changes. A protocol that is safe under one model can be unsafe under another.
</Callout>

<Callout variant="lab" title="Identity provider compromise">
A central identity provider can authenticate users to every connected service. Compromising it is equivalent to compromising many systems at once. A decentralised identity design may reduce this blast radius, but it introduces recovery, revocation and interoperability problems. The right architecture depends on which failure is worse for the organisation.

### Define the properties precisely

**Safety** means that nothing bad happens: two honest replicas never commit conflicting values at the same log position. **Liveness** means that valid requests eventually commit once the network is stable and enough replicas participate. **Validity** requires a committed value to have been proposed by a correct participant. These properties can be proven separately; a safe but halted system has not failed the same way as one that commits fraud.

A quorum system is a collection $Q$ of subsets. It is consistent when any two quorums intersect, and available when at least one member of $Q$ is contained in every possible non-faulty replica set. With classical crash faults, majority quorums of size $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$ suffice. With Byzantine faults, the larger vote size matters because intersecting replicas may themselves be faulty. For $n=3f+1$ and quorum size $q=2f+1$,

$$
|Q_1 \cap Q_2| \ge 2q-n = f+1.
$$

Since at most $f$ replicas are faulty, at least one replica in every intersection is honest. Honest replicas do not vote for conflicting values at the same position, which is the core safety argument. This is an assumption-driven result: authenticated point-to-point messages, identifiable participants, known membership and a static or carefully managed fault bound are part of the model. <Cite id="lamport1982" locator="pp. 382–401" />

The bound $n \ge 3f+1$ is not a law for every consensus mechanism. Signed messages, trusted hardware, randomised protocols and economic consensus change the assumptions and trade-offs. Adding replicas increases fault tolerance only if they are independent and the protocol is configured for the new membership.

CAP requires its own precision. Linearizability means that every operation appears to take effect atomically at one instant between its invocation and response. It is stronger than eventual consistency and different from serialisability of database transactions. The partition argument applies when a client cannot distinguish an unavailable replica from a slow one. It does **not** imply that a healthy database always sacrifices consistency, or that every read must fail when one replica is isolated. Apply the theorem per operation and state the consistency contract visible to clients. <Cite id="gilbert-lynch2002" locator="Vol. 33, No. 2, pp. 51–59" />

A final edge case is external truth. Consensus proves that a state transition followed agreed rules; it cannot prove that an input was accurate. Put provenance, sanity bounds and dispute windows around external inputs that can move money or alter safety-critical decisions.
</Callout>

<Lens level={4} title="Operating a distributed trust boundary">

Failure modes: split brain, validator churn, key compromise, upgrade deadlock, bridge/oracle risk and client monoculture.

Controls:

1. Explicit fault model: crash, Byzantine, coercion, insider.
2. Key management: HSMs, threshold signatures, multi-party approval and rotation.
3. Independent observability of consensus, peers and finality.
4. Documented governance, emergency and dispute processes.
5. Tested disaster recovery and state reconstruction.
6. Reproducible builds, signed releases and client diversity.

<Callout variant="danger" title="Decentralisation is not a compliance strategy">
Distributing control complicates accountability and data protection. You still need lawful processing, incident response and deletion where required. “The blockchain did it” is not an incident-response plan.
</Callout>

Choose centralisation when you need low latency, strong consistency and clear accountability. Choose decentralisation when no single party should be able to censor or rewrite history, and the application tolerates weaker finality. Most systems are hybrids; test each component's failure.

## Failure modes and controls

| Failure | Impact | Control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Control-plane compromise | global state or policy change | least privilege, hardware-backed keys, break-glass audit |
| Split brain | conflicting state | quorum rules, fencing tokens, partition testing |
| Validator capture | censorship or rewrite | membership policy, geographic/organisational diversity |
| Upgrade capture | malicious code becomes law | reproducible builds, multi-party release signing |
| Key loss | account or validator loss | threshold custody, recovery policy |
| Client monoculture | one bug halts everyone | independent implementations, canary releases |

## Measuring decentralisation

Ask how many independent entities can censor a transaction, finalise a block, change the protocol or read the data. Count validator/stake concentration, client diversity, hosting concentration and governance control. A system with a central upgrade key and one hosted RPC provider is not meaningfully decentralised, even if it uses a blockchain.

## Choosing an architecture

Use centralisation when you need low latency, strong consistency, clear accountability and easy recovery. Use decentralisation when no single party should be able to censor or rewrite history, and the application tolerates weaker finality and higher coordination cost. Most systems are hybrids; document the boundary and test failure.

### Deployment and observability

Run validators as independent failure domains, but do not confuse geographic separation with control independence. Measure CPU saturation, disk write latency, network delay, view-change rate, queue depth, vote age and checkpoint divergence for every replica. A healthy aggregate dashboard can hide one validator repeatedly timing out. Export event time, ingestion time, validator identity, view number, block height and quorum-certificate status. Alert on missing quorum certificates, not merely missing blocks, because a server may be producing blocks that clients cannot verify.

Set performance budgets from the product contract. If settlement must complete within five seconds for 99.9% of requests, measure from client submission through validation, consensus, persistence and receipt. The timeout must exceed realistic network and disk tails, yet be short enough to recover from a failed leader. Test with packet loss, clock skew, garbage-collection pauses and a slow disk on one validator.

Use canary validators for new code and expand only after checkpoint equality, latency and error checks pass. Software rollback is usually safe for the consensus binary; a state-machine rollback requires a protocol-level decision and can violate auditability. Exercise that distinction.

### Operational readiness checklist

Fault injection should cover crashes during every phase of a proposal, delayed and reordered messages, duplicate votes, equivocation, clock drift, disk exhaustion and loss of a trust anchor. Partition the network into all relevant splits rather than only a simple majority/minority division. Verify that clients detect stale reads and that recovery reconstructs state from certificates, not gossip. Run key-rotation and membership-change drills under load. A backup is not proven until a separate environment has restored it and verified its final certificate.

1. Record the fault model, independence assumptions and per-operation consistency contract.
2. Size quorums for the stated Byzantine and availability budgets, then test the arithmetic against actual membership.
3. Separate proposer, validator-admission, upgrade-signing and recovery authorities.
4. Replicate validators across independent power, network, cloud and administrative domains.
5. Protect signing keys with hardware or threshold custody and rehearse rotation and compromise response.
6. Monitor quorum progress, state divergence and external oracle freshness.
7. Benchmark p95 and p99 latency under loss, slow disks and clock skew.
8. Test partition recovery, state reconstruction, software rollback and emergency membership changes.
9. Verify that monitoring and governance tooling do not introduce a single hidden administrator.
10. Review the smallest coalition able to censor, rewrite, recover or upgrade after every membership change.

## Common failure modes

| Failure mode | Observable symptom | Root cause | Guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Apparent centralisation | All validators share a cloud account or admin identity | Independence measured by server count | Map control-plane ownership and hosting concentration |
| Split brain | Two sites report different final heights | Quorum or fencing rule misconfigured | Require certificates and reject writes without quorum |
| Validator capture | Repeated censorship or membership changes by one coalition | Admission and transaction authority combined | Separate charters, thresholds and emergency powers |
| Upgrade deadlock | Validators run incompatible protocol versions | No activation window or compatibility test | Version negotiation, canaries and staged activation |
| Key loss | A validator silently stops participating | Single-copy key and no recovery drill | Threshold custody and tested replacement |

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Block height advances but clients cannot verify receipts | Quorum certificates are missing or produced by a minority | Check participant membership, signature thresholds and certificate distribution |
| Frequent leader changes during peak load | Timeout below network or disk tail latency | Capture latency percentiles, repair slow replicas and retune conservatively |
| Rejoining replica never catches up | Checkpoint trust anchor mismatch or missing log range | Verify anchor hashes and restore the certified range from independent peers |
| Recovery key works without quorum | Backup workflow bypasses threshold policy | Remove implicit full-key export and require multi-party authorisation |
| Network heals but two histories persist | Both sides accepted an uncertified extension | Stop clients, identify the certified prefix and discard uncertified tails |
| Monitoring says healthy during censorship | Dashboard follows throughput, not request outcomes | Track rejected valid requests, per-client completion and proposer concentration |

## Design review checklist

- [ ] The largest Byzantine and unavailable sets are stated separately, with quorum arithmetic shown.
- [ ] Every operation has an explicit consistency and retry contract during partition.
- [ ] Validator independence includes hosting, networking, administrators and key material.
- [ ] Quorum intersection protects safety under the stated authentication and membership model.
- [ ] External data has provenance, bounds and a dispute path rather than being treated as consensus truth.
- [ ] Membership, upgrade, recovery and emergency authorities have distinct approvers.
- [ ] Split-brain, equivocation, clock-skew and slow-disk tests have expected safety outcomes.
- [ ] Observability shows certificate progress and divergence per independent entity, not only uptime.

## Key takeaways

- Centralisation concentrates control and trust; decentralisation redistributes them but adds coordination costs.
- CAP and BFT bounds constrain what a distributed design can promise.
- Recovery, identity and upgrade authority are security-critical design choices.
- Quorum intersection and membership policy determine Byzantine safety.
- “Decentralised” is a measurable spectrum, not a binary label.
