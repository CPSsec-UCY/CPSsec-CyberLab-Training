## The promise

Federated learning lets many clients train a shared model while keeping their raw data local. A phone keyboard, a hospital or a bank sends a model update instead of records. The server aggregates updates and returns a new global model.

That reduces data concentration, which is a real security and privacy benefit. It does **not** make the data invisible. Updates can leak information, and a malicious participant can still poison the shared model.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Privacy is not integrity">
Secure aggregation can hide an individual update from the server while a poisoned update still corrupts the model. Treat privacy and integrity as separate threat models with separate controls.
</Callout>

## The cast

- **Clients** hold local data and compute updates.
- **Server / aggregator** coordinates rounds and combines updates.
- **Attacker** may be a curious server, a malicious client, a network observer or a model consumer.

## Why it matters

Hospitals cannot ship patient records to a central vendor. A federated design can still learn a useful model while keeping records inside the hospital boundary. The trade-off is communication, heterogeneity and a larger attack surface for poisoning and inference. <Cite id="mcmahan2017" locator="pp. 1273–1282" />

## A concrete deployment

Three hospitals want a model that predicts a rare complication. Each has a few hundred labelled cases; none has enough alone, and none can legally export patient records. Federated averaging lets each hospital train locally and share only model updates. The model improves, but the consortium must still answer:

- Can the aggregator infer anything about an individual patient from an update?
- Can one hospital poison the shared model?
- What happens when a hospital drops out mid-round?
- Who owns the final model, and who may query it?

These are governance and security questions, not just machine-learning questions.

### Threat model: two attackers with different goals

Separate the curious aggregator from the malicious participant. The curious aggregator may be contractually restricted but still technically able to inspect updates, timing, client identities and released models. Its incentive is to improve a service or infer commercially valuable information. The malicious client wants the global model to misclassify a trigger, leak membership information, gain disproportionate influence or waste the consortium's resources. A network observer may learn participation and model version even without reading encrypted updates. A model consumer can submit chosen queries and exploit overfitting. Each capability requires a separate control; hiding one update from the server does not stop a poisoned aggregate.

In an eight-hospital deployment, a rare-condition model is trained for two hundred rounds. Each hospital benefits if accuracy improves, but a hospital with unusual equipment also wants its local performance preserved. That creates incentives to free-ride, distort data or waste resources. Secure aggregation reduces the aggregator's visibility, yet it also makes outlier inspection harder without an authorised verification path.

Several intuitive claims are wrong. Keeping records local does not mean updates are anonymous: gradients or parameter differences can encode examples. Adding noise once does not give a fixed lifetime privacy guarantee, because repeated releases compose. Hiding individual updates does not mean the final aggregate is safe: ten colluding clients can target the same feature. More clients do not automatically reduce poisoning; a large attacker-controlled population can increase both weight and confidence. A good threat-model statement names the privacy unit, the collusion threshold, the number of adversarial updates and whether the server follows the protocol.

Define success before training: clinical utility, bounded privacy loss, maximum poisoning impact and round availability. Reject a candidate if clean validation accuracy falls by more than two percentage points or any protected subgroup falls by more than five points. These policy thresholds turn concerns into testable release gates.

### Write an adversary specification, not just a list of attackers

“Malicious client” and “curious server” are starting points, not complete models. A testable specification names the **privacy unit**, the neighbouring relation, the maximum colluding set, the parties whose endpoints may be compromised, the server's allowed deviations and every release visible to an outsider. At record level, neighbours differ by one example. At client level, they differ by one client's entire contribution. At update level, the protected object is a bounded vector; that claim alone says nothing about all records held by its owner. The unit must match the promise made to the person whose data is being protected.

Next distinguish passive from active behaviour. An honest-but-curious aggregator follows the protocol but inspects shares, timing, identities and released models. A malicious aggregator may omit selected clients, substitute a model version, alter the clipping instruction or withhold output. Secure aggregation may hide individual vectors from both, but availability and correct model publication need additional evidence. Likewise, a malicious client can send malformed shares, abort after contributing, submit stale rounds or collude with other clients; a compromised endpoint can exfiltrate raw data before clipping occurs. Encryption on the wire cannot protect an update computed on an already compromised device.

Collusion should be bounded independently of dropout. If a protocol reconstructs from six of eight clients, that number describes availability. It does not tell you how many clients may pool their views before an individual update is exposed. A secret-sharing construction has its own collusion threshold, and a robust aggregator has a separate corruption budget. Mixing these numbers produces a false guarantee such as “six-of-eight is private against six attackers.”

Map the data flow as an attack graph: enrolment, local dataset, model download, training, clipping, masking, share submission, reconstruction, noise, evaluation, publication and downstream queries. At each edge record who can observe, modify, replay or suppress the artefact. Participation timing can leak membership, a model version can reveal the selected cohort, and a public query log can become a second dataset. Controls placed only on gradient upload leave these channels open.

Governance authority belongs in the same specification. Decide who may change the objective, approve a client population, read diagnostic shares, set the privacy budget, revoke an organisation and publish a model. A coordinator that lowers clipping or resets accounting can invalidate privacy and integrity while cryptographic checks pass. The release gate needs signed configuration, append-only accounting, independent evaluation and named approval authority.

Finally, separate worst-case guarantees from empirical tests. Differential privacy gives a distributional guarantee for the declared neighbours; secure aggregation gives confidentiality under its cryptographic assumptions; neither promises useful accuracy or resistance to every poisoning strategy. Release documentation should label each claim as proved, measured or assumed.

## FedAvg in one loop

1. The server selects a subset of clients.
2. Each client trains locally for some steps.
3. Clients send model updates (or parameters).
4. The server averages them, weighted by sample count.
5. The new global model is distributed and the round repeats.

<Cite id="mcmahan2017" locator="pp. 1273–1282" />

## The privacy controls

- **Secure aggregation:** clients mask updates so the server sees only the sum. It handles dropout with secret sharing. <Cite id="bonawitz2017" locator="pp. 1175–1191" />
- **Differential privacy:** add calibrated noise or clip contributions so the output does not reveal much about any single record. <Cite id="dwork-roth2014" locator="Vol. 9, pp. 211–407" /> <Cite id="abadi2016" locator="pp. 308–318" />
- **Local privacy:** clients perturb before sending, protecting against the server but usually reducing utility more.
- **Access control and attestation:** authenticate clients and verify the code that computes updates.

## What can still go wrong

- **Poisoning:** a client sends an update that degrades accuracy or installs a backdoor. <Cite id="bagdasaryan2020" locator="pp. 2938–2948" />
- **Membership inference:** an attacker tests whether a record was in the training set. <Cite id="shokri2017" locator="pp. 3–18" />
- **Sybil attacks:** one adversary controls many fake clients.
- **Free riding:** clients submit useless updates to receive the model.
- **Gradient leakage:** updates can reveal training examples in some settings.

<Callout variant="danger" title="A privacy budget is a security parameter">
Differential privacy composes: every release consumes budget. Repeated rounds, small client counts and weak clipping make the guarantee fragile. Document epsilon, delta, clipping and the threat model. <Cite id="dwork-roth2014" locator="Vol. 9, pp. 211–407" />
</Callout>



## FedAvg and the privacy stack in detail

In a round, the server samples clients, sends the current model, and each selected client performs local SGD. The server then computes a weighted average:

$$
w_{t+1} = \sum_{k \in S_t} \frac{n_k}{\sum_{j \in S_t} n_j} w_{t+1}^k.
$$

Local training can overfit, so the update may encode more about the client's data than intended. Three controls are often combined:

1. **Clipping:** bound the norm of each update so one client cannot dominate.
2. **Secure aggregation:** mask updates so the server sees only the sum. The protocol must tolerate clients leaving mid-round, typically using secret sharing and a dropout threshold. <Cite id="bonawitz2017" locator="pp. 1175–1191" />
3. **Differential privacy:** add noise calibrated to the clipping norm and privacy budget. Privacy composes across rounds, so a long run consumes more budget. <Cite id="dwork-roth2014" locator="Vol. 9, pp. 211–407" />

<Callout variant="warning" title="Secure aggregation is not a correctness check">
The server may be unable to see an individual update, but it still incorporates that update into the sum. A poisoned or low-quality update can degrade the model. Robust aggregation, reputation and validation are separate controls.
</Callout>

## A worked round

Suppose four clients have 420, 310, 580 and 260 samples. The weighted average gives the largest client about 37% of the update and the smallest about 17%. A malicious client with 5,000 samples could dominate unless the server caps per-client influence. That is why contribution bounds and clipping are security controls, not only privacy controls.

If one client drops out during secure aggregation, the server needs enough remaining shares to reconstruct the sum. The dropout threshold, timeout and recovery behaviour are part of the protocol design.

### Follow one round end to end

The coordinator checks authentication, approved training code, model version and device readiness, then samples clients with a documented policy. Uniform sampling supports an unbiased estimate; favouring large or fast clients changes it. The server sends the model hash, round identifier, clipping norm, noise configuration and deadline.

A client freezes the model version, runs deterministic local steps, clips the update and applies local privacy. It creates masked shares and signs a commitment. A client leaving after usable shares may still be included; one leaving earlier is a dropout, not silently zero unless the protocol says so.

The aggregator verifies commitments, reconstructs only after the threshold holds, divides by included sample counts, applies global noise and records privacy accounting. Evaluate on an independently governed held-out set, not only easy-to-reach clients. Publish a signed model manifest and await acknowledgements before the next round.

### What a secure sum hides

A simplified secure-sum protocol shows the core cancellation property. Work in a finite field or fixed-width ring and let client $i$ hold update vector $v_i$. For every pair of clients, choose a random mask $r_{ij}$ and set $r_{ji}=-r_{ij}$. Client $i$ submits

$$
y_i = v_i + \sum_{j \ne i} r_{ij}.
$$

If every selected client submits exactly once, the pairwise terms cancel:

$$
\sum_i y_i = \sum_i v_i.
$$

The server learns the sum but not an individual vector, provided the masks remain unknown and clients do not reveal their views. This illustration assumes authenticated clients, secure channels, honest endpoints and a fixed participant set. It is not the complete Bonawitz protocol. Production constructions add secret-shared masks, double masking, commitments and verification so that malicious clients cannot submit inconsistent shares. <Cite id="bonawitz2017" locator="pp. 1180–1187" />

Dropout breaks the simple cancellation equation because a missing client also removes its masks. Substituting a zero update is generally wrong: the sum then contains an unknown mask offset rather than the intended dropout-free aggregate. Threshold secret sharing lets surviving participants recover the missing mask contributions without revealing honest updates. The protocol must still record which contributions are usable, preserve the agreed weighting and refuse to publish if reconstruction or validation fails. Failure robustness means a defined recovery or abort path; it does not mean every dropout can be hidden without cost.

Commitments differ from encryption. They bind a client to a masked share before reconstruction, preventing adaptation after seeing others. Authentication binds the share to an identity and round. Range and dimension checks prevent wraparound or malformed vectors from corrupting the sum. These checks must use the protocol's specified representation, not a server-side plaintext update the privacy design was meant to hide.

## The optimisation view

Federated learning minimises a weighted objective over local losses:

$$
\min_w \; F(w) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{n_k}{n} F_k(w),
$$

where $F_k$ is client $k$'s local objective. FedAvg approximates this by local SGD and periodic averaging; heterogeneity and partial participation introduce bias. <Cite id="kairouz2021" locator="Vol. 14, pp. 1–210" />

## Differential privacy

A randomised mechanism $M$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP if for neighbouring datasets $D, D'$ and every event $S$:

$$
\Pr[M(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[M(D') \in S] + \delta.
$$

In federated learning, DP can be applied at the record, client or update level. Each has different meaning and composition. Clipping bounds sensitivity; noise scales with sensitivity and the privacy budget. <Cite id="abadi2016" locator="pp. 308–318" />

## Secure aggregation

Clients secret-share masked updates; the server reconstructs only the sum if enough shares are available. Dropout tolerance requires threshold secret sharing and careful round management. Security holds against a curious server and colluding clients below the threshold. <Cite id="bonawitz2017" locator="pp. 1175–1191" />

<Callout variant="lab" title="Privacy and integrity compose badly">
A secure aggregation protocol can protect a poisoned update from inspection. Robust aggregation, contribution bounds, anomaly detection and client reputation are separate defences. Do not assume one control covers both properties.
</Callout>

## Membership inference

A model can memorise. Membership inference exploits differences in confidence between training and non-training examples. DP and regularisation reduce the signal, but they do not prove that no inference is possible. <Cite id="shokri2017" locator="pp. 3–18" />



## What the formal guarantees do and do not say

<Figure caption="Privacy and integrity are separate axes. Secure aggregation improves confidentiality but does not validate the update; robust aggregation and DP address different risks.">
  <FederatedThreatMatrix />
</Figure>

Differential privacy bounds the influence of one record on the output. Record-level DP, client-level DP and update-level DP have different meanings; a claim is meaningless without the unit of privacy and the neighbouring-dataset relation. Clipping bounds sensitivity; noise scales with sensitivity and budget.

Secure aggregation protects updates against a curious server and colluding clients below a threshold. It does not protect against a malicious client poisoning the sum, a compromised client device, or a server that can observe the final model and query it.

Membership inference exploits differences in model behaviour between training and non-training records. <Cite id="shokri2017" locator="pp. 3–18" /> DP and regularisation reduce the signal, but a released model can still leak through queries, confidence scores and repeated access.

## Poisoning and backdoors

An attacker can scale an update to dominate the average, target a trigger pattern, or collude across clients. Backdoors are especially difficult because the model behaves normally on clean inputs. Defences include clipping, robust aggregation (for example coordinate-wise median), anomaly detection, contribution bounds, client reputation and independent evaluation. <Cite id="bagdasaryan2020" locator="pp. 2938–2948" />

No single defence covers all attackers. The threat model must state whether clients can be malicious, whether the server is honest-but-curious, and whether Sybil identities are cheap.

### Integrity controls have different failure modes

Clipping limits the **mass** of an attack but not its direction. Suppose $p$ is the total aggregation weight of malicious clients and each clipped update has norm at most $C$. The norm of their combined contribution is at most $pC$. In a 100-client round, ten malicious clients with equal weight can contribute at most one tenth of the clipping norm in their chosen direction. That bound does not prove the attack is harmless: clean updates may be small, heterogeneous or cancel, and a targeted feature may be sensitive to a much smaller perturbation. If enrolment is cheap, the attacker can raise $p$ by creating Sybil clients, so client-level clipping does not replace Sybil resistance.

Model-replacement backdoors exploit this structure. Instead of poisoning local training data and hoping a small gradient survives, the attacker trains toward a trigger task and scales the resulting update to the allowed norm. Clipping may constrain the final vector but does not remove the targeted direction. The constrain-and-scale attack explicitly optimises for persistence and evasion, so an outlier detector must be evaluated against defence-aware attacks rather than only random noise. <Cite id="bagdasaryan2020" locator="§4.2–4.3" />

Robust aggregation changes the failure budget. A coordinate-wise median can tolerate fewer than half malicious values in a coordinate under the usual symmetric-error assumptions, but corruption spread across coordinates can evade per-coordinate reasoning. A trimmed mean requires a stated trim fraction and an upper bound on attacker weight. Both can discard legitimate updates under heavy non-IID heterogeneity. Their security therefore depends on the data distribution, coordinate correlations and attacker strategy, not only the aggregator name.

| Control | Hides individual updates? | Bounds update magnitude? | Resists malicious direction? | Detects a hidden backdoor? | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Secure aggregation | Yes, under its collusion model | No | No | No | Malicious contributions still enter the sum |
| Clipping | No | Yes | No | No | Many bounded attackers can align |
| Gaussian DP | No | Works with a sensitivity bound | No | No | Noise can reduce utility; budget composes |
| Coordinate median or trimmed mean | No | Often reduces extreme influence | Only within a corruption bound | Not by itself | Heterogeneity can reject honest updates |
| Sybil-resistant enrolment | No | Indirectly limits attacker weight | Indirectly | No | Does not stop a compromised legitimate client |
| Independent clean and subgroup evaluation | No | No | Supports rollback | Can reveal targeted regression | Requires representative evaluation data |

Membership inference has its own evaluation loop. A black-box attacker can train shadow models, query the target on members and non-members, and classify confidence or loss patterns without reading training updates. Defences include reducing overfitting, limiting queries, calibrating confidence, regularisation and a privacy guarantee covering the released model; secure aggregation does not address the serving interface. <Cite id="shokri2017" locator="§V and §VIII" />

Test the defence suite jointly. Measure clean accuracy, worst-subgroup accuracy, backdoor success, membership-inference advantage, reconstruction failures and communication cost together. Noise can hide a backdoor from anomaly detection, secure aggregation can prevent individual inspection, and robust aggregation can bias results against a minority hospital; the release decision is a multi-objective gate.

### Privacy units, sensitivity and composition

The word privacy is incomplete until the unit is named. Record-level DP limits the effect of adding or removing one example; client-level DP limits the effect of one client's entire dataset; update-level DP may protect a single vector contribution but not the underlying client. Neighbouring datasets must differ only under the declared relation. Client-level DP is usually the relevant claim when each hospital contributes one bounded update, but it is not interchangeable with record-level DP. <Cite id="dwork-roth2014" locator="Vol. 9, pp. 211–407" />

For a vector-valued query with $L_2$ sensitivity $C$, the Gaussian mechanism adds independent Gaussian noise to each coordinate. For the analytic calibration with $0<\varepsilon<1$, choose $c^2>2\ln(1.25/\delta)$ and

$$
\sigma \ge \frac{cC}{\varepsilon}.
$$

Here $C$ must be the sensitivity of the **released query after clipping and normalisation**, not merely the largest observed gradient. If one client's update is clipped to norm $C$ and contributions are averaged, the aggregate's sensitivity depends on that client's maximum weight. Changing sample weighting, adding clients or omitting dropouts can change the denominator and therefore the sensitivity analysis. The formula is valid only for the specified mechanism, privacy unit and neighbouring relation; it is not a recipe for an arbitrary training objective. <Cite id="dwork-roth2014" locator="§3.5.3, Theorem 3.22" />

With independent releases, basic composition gives $\varepsilon_{\text{total}}\le \sum_r \varepsilon_r$ and $\delta_{\text{total}}\le \sum_r \delta_r$. Advanced composition can be tighter in some regimes, but it must be evaluated with its actual formula and parameters. For per-round parameters $(\varepsilon,\delta_{\mathrm{round}})$, a generic advanced bound is $\varepsilon' \le \sqrt{2T\ln(1/\delta')}\,\varepsilon + T\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon-1)$ with total $\delta\le T\delta_{\mathrm{round}}+\delta'$; it is not always smaller than basic composition. Specialised moments accountants exploit the Gaussian mechanism and sampling process and can be substantially tighter for private training, but they require a validated implementation and a complete record of sampling, clipping and noise settings. <Cite id="dwork-roth2014" locator="§3.5.1–3.5.2" /> <Cite id="abadi2016" locator="§3.1–3.2" />

DP guarantees are worst-case: they say the output distributions change little for neighbours, not that every record is impossible to infer. Post-processing cannot increase privacy, but a released model plus side information can still support attacks outside the protected unit. Adaptive composition matters when later rounds or releases are chosen using earlier outputs. Random sampling can amplify privacy, but only under the sampling assumption used by the analysis; adversarially selecting a favourable cohort does not inherit the same guarantee.

### Correctness and robustness are separate arguments

Secure aggregation must reconstruct the intended sum despite missing shares and malicious contributions. Correctness assumes authenticated shares, consistent dimensions, correct commitments and a compatible reconstruction threshold. Confidentiality limits collusion; neither property establishes that the sum is useful or benign. <Cite id="bonawitz2017" locator="pp. 1175–1191" />

A coordinate-wise median has a breakdown point of one half for one coordinate, but coordinated poisoning across coordinates or heterogeneous data can defeat it. A trimmed mean requires a bound on the attacker population. Clipping limits magnitude, not direction, so many bounded malicious clients can still bias an average.

FedAvg is an optimisation approximation. If selected clients draw from the weighted population objective and use sufficiently small steps, averaging can approximate central SGD. Partial participation, local overfitting and changing distributions introduce bias and variance. Edge cases include zero samples, non-finite values, repeated client identifiers and updates for the wrong round.

## Operational realities

- **Heterogeneity:** clients have different data distributions, compute and availability.
- **Dropout:** secure aggregation must not fail when a client disappears mid-round.
- **Communication cost:** model updates are large; compression and quantisation change privacy and poisoning behaviour.
- **Versioning:** clients may run stale code or malicious forks.
- **Attestation:** verify the client environment when the threat model includes compromised devices.
- **Monitoring:** measure participation, update norms and accuracy; alert on drift and outliers.

## Controls to combine

1. Client authentication and Sybil resistance.
2. Secure channels and signed updates.
3. Clipping and DP noise with a documented budget.
4. Robust aggregation (median, trimmed mean) where appropriate.
5. Reputation and contribution bounds.
6. Independent evaluation on a held-out, non-federated dataset.
7. Incident response for poisoned rounds and model rollback.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Do not promise more than the model proves">
“Federated” is not a compliance certificate. Data minimisation, retention, legal basis and user transparency still apply. A privacy-preserving training pipeline can still produce a privacy-invasive deployed model.
</Callout>

## Systems and operations

- **Heterogeneity:** clients differ in data distribution, compute, connectivity and availability.
- **Dropout:** secure aggregation must reconstruct the sum if enough clients remain; thresholds and timeouts are protocol parameters.
- **Communication:** updates can be large; compression and quantisation interact with privacy and poisoning.
- **Versioning:** clients may run stale code, and a malicious fork can change update semantics.
- **Monitoring:** measure participation, update norms and model behaviour without collecting personal data.

## Testing checklist

1. Simulate poisoning clients and measure detection and impact.
2. Audit DP accounting and clipping under realistic distributions.
3. Test dropout and reconstruction paths in secure aggregation.
4. Run membership-inference evaluations on the released model.
5. Verify rollback when a round introduces a regression or backdoor.
6. Document the privacy unit, budget, threat model and trust boundary.

<Callout variant="danger" title="Federated is not a compliance certificate">
Data minimisation, lawful basis, retention and user transparency still apply. A federated training pipeline can still produce a privacy-invasive deployed model, and model updates can be personal data.

### Deployment patterns and performance

A production coordinator is a state machine, not a notebook. Persist the round identifier, selected client commitments, included contributors, model hash, clipping and noise parameters, privacy accountant state and evaluation result before publishing a model. Make publication idempotent so a retry cannot consume the privacy budget twice. Separate the training plane from the public model-serving plane: an attacker who can query the model may be different from the curious aggregator that trained it.

Communication often dominates runtime. A 10-million-parameter model uses about 40 MB in 32-bit floating point and 10 MB after 8-bit quantisation. To 1,000 clients, download and upload together consume about 80 GB or 20 GB before overhead. Sparse updates and compression reduce traffic but can change clipping, noise and poisoning behaviour; verify exact encode/decode equivalence.

Schedule clients with backpressure and stagger uploads to avoid a deadline thundering herd. Cache the global model by hash and reject stale contributions. On constrained phones, defer training to charging and unmetered conditions; that policy affects population representation. Track broad participation cohorts rather than extra personal data.

### Observability, testing and response

Useful round telemetry includes eligible and selected clients, submission and dropout rates, deadline percentiles, reconstruction failures, update norms before and after clipping, non-finite-value rate, training loss aggregates and candidate-versus-baseline utility. Privacy accounting is a hard gate: a failed accountant must stop publication, not emit a warning. Anomaly dashboards should avoid displaying raw gradients or unique client-level metrics unless an authorised analysis process justifies them.

Test with honest heterogeneity and adversarial clients simultaneously. Include Byzantine scaling, feature-colluding backdoors, Sybil identities, stale code, duplicated rounds, malformed shares and dropouts at every protocol boundary. Run membership-inference and subgroup-utility evaluations on every candidate class of change. Verify that a rollback restores the previous signed model and privacy-accountant checkpoint without rerunning the poisoned round.

The round runbook should define who can pause training, invalidate a model, revoke a participant, preserve privacy accounting and communicate regressions. Rehearse a poisoned-round response before production.

1. Pin and attest the training code and objective configuration.
2. Authenticate clients and make Sybil enrolment materially costly.
3. Record the privacy unit, clipping norm, total epsilon and delta before training.
4. Enforce dropout, deadline and idempotency rules in the coordinator state machine.
5. Validate finite values, dimensions, round identity and update commitments.
6. Measure traffic and p95 round completion under realistic client constraints.
7. Monitor aggregate quality and participation without collecting unnecessary raw data.
8. Test poisoning, dropout, stale-model and reconstruction-failure paths.
9. Gate release on privacy accounting and independent evaluation.
10. Rehearse model rollback, participant revocation and consumer notification.

### Make the coordinator's powers explicit

The coordinator is a privileged actor even when it cannot see individual updates. It chooses eligibility, sampling, model version, clipping parameters, deadlines, reconstruction thresholds and publication time. It can also delay a round, omit a valid contribution or release a stale model. A secure-aggregation guarantee should therefore be paired with a coordinator threat model: what can it learn, what can it change, and what evidence can another party verify? Signed configuration, append-only round records and independent release approval constrain these powers without requiring plaintext gradients.

Three thresholds belong in every protocol specification. The **reconstruction threshold** determines how many usable contributions are needed to recover the sum. The **collusion threshold** determines how many parties may pool views before update confidentiality fails. The **corruption threshold** determines how many malicious updates the integrity rule is designed to tolerate. For eight clients, six-of-eight reconstruction tolerates two dropouts; a sharing scheme with a two-party collusion bound does not become safer because six shares are required; and a robust aggregator may tolerate a different number of malicious vectors. Support each threshold with its experiment or theorem.

Monitoring must not recreate the surveillance the protocol was meant to prevent. Aggregate norms and participation rates can reveal unusual clients in small cohorts. Restrict client-level diagnostics to an authorised audit process with purpose and retention limits. Allow a separately authorised quorum to inspect shares for a bounded investigation while the routine aggregator sees only the aggregate, and test that audit path for collusion and logging leaks.

A poisoned round is a security incident. Preserve the last signed model and accounting checkpoint, stop publication, identify affected consumers and separate observed regressions from causal hypotheses. Define who can pause training, revoke a participant, authorise diagnostics and approve a rerun. These actions map to incident-response preparation, detection, containment and recovery, with evidence handling adapted to private training. <Cite id="sp800-61r3" locator="§2–5" /> Do not reset the privacy accountant on rollback: computation already occurred, and diagnostics may consume more budget.

The serving plane needs a separate review. A privately trained model can expose membership through confidence scores or repeated queries. Apply query authentication, rate limits, output calibration and release quotas. Include published model-derived statistics in the privacy ledger. A model card should record population, privacy unit, budget, subgroup limitations, poisoning tests and revocation procedure.

Exercise combined failures: heterogeneous honest clients, two dropouts, a stale client, a scaled backdoor and a delayed share. Verify that reconstruction respects the threshold, the integrity rule behaves as analysed, the accountant records actual releases and rollback restores the previous signed artefacts.

## Common failure modes

| Failure mode | Observable symptom | Root cause | Guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Privacy claim without a unit | Team says DP but cannot define neighbours | Record, client and update privacy conflated | Declare unit, relation and total budget |
| Budget reset each round | Total epsilon grows unnoticed | Releases accounted independently | Persistent accountant and hard publication gate |
| Hidden poisoning | Aggregate looks plausible but targeted accuracy falls | Confidentiality mistaken for integrity | Robust aggregation and subgroup evaluation |
| Selection bias | Model improves only for easy or fast clients | Scheduling favours convenient devices | Track representative participation cohorts |
| Dropout deadlock | Rounds repeatedly abort | Reconstruction threshold too high | Tune threshold and timeout against observed dropout |

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aggregate cannot be reconstructed | Too many clients left before usable shares | Verify threshold, deadlines and share acknowledgements; abort safely |
| Global accuracy falls after a round | Poisoning, drift or excessive clipping | Roll back, compare cohorts and run authorised diagnostics |
| Privacy accountant rejects publication | Budget exhausted or metadata inconsistent | Stop releases, reconcile round history and reallocate remaining budget |
| Client uploads fail with dimension errors | Model version or quantisation mismatch | Check signed model hash and deterministic encode/decode tests |
| Slow clients never participate | Deadline or sampling policy excludes them | Stagger scheduling and measure cohort participation |
| Utility collapses when epsilon shrinks | Noise too large for clipped signal | Revisit total budget, architecture and privacy unit rather than bypassing DP |

## Design review checklist

- [ ] The privacy unit, neighbouring relation, epsilon, delta and composition method are explicit.
- [ ] Secure aggregation's dropout threshold and collusion threshold are stated separately.
- [ ] Every client update is bound to a signed round and pinned training configuration.
- [ ] Clipping, robust aggregation and Sybil controls have documented limits.
- [ ] Evaluation includes clean data, subgroup slices and poisoning simulations.
- [ ] Communication and completion budgets include both model download and update upload.
- [ ] The coordinator persists privacy accounting and model manifests idempotently.
- [ ] Monitoring avoids raw updates and unnecessary client-level personal data.
- [ ] A rollback restores the previous model and accountant state without republishing.

## Key takeaways

- Federated learning reduces raw-data centralisation but does not automatically provide privacy or integrity.

- Secure aggregation protects update confidentiality, not correctness.
- Differential privacy needs a unit, a budget and composition accounting.
- Poisoning and backdoors require separate robust-aggregation and monitoring controls.
- Operational details — dropout, heterogeneity, client authentication — decide whether the system works.
