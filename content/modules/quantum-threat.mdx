## The short version

Two quantum algorithms change the security landscape, and they do not do the same thing.

- **Shor's algorithm** solves integer factorisation and discrete logarithms efficiently. It does not weaken RSA or elliptic-curve cryptography; it removes their underlying hardness assumption entirely. <Cite id="shor1994" locator="pp. 124–134" />
- **Grover's algorithm** gives a quadratic speed-up for unstructured search. It halves the effective security level of symmetric keys and hash preimages, but it does not break AES or SHA-2 outright. <Cite id="grover1996" locator="pp. 212–219" />

That distinction drives everything that follows. RSA, finite-field Diffie–Hellman, ECDH, ECDSA and EdDSA need replacement. AES and SHA need larger parameters, not a new design. NIST's 2016 report already framed the problem this way. <Cite id="nist-ir8105" locator="§4–5" />

## The attacker you should actually worry about

A cryptographically relevant quantum computer does not need to exist today for the threat to be real. An adversary can record encrypted traffic now and decrypt it later. For data with a long confidentiality lifetime — health records, source code, diplomatic cables, firmware signing keys — "later" is inside the risk window.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Harvest now, decrypt later">
The migration deadline is not the date a quantum computer appears. It is the date when the data you protected today is still sensitive, minus the time it takes to migrate. For a 25-year secret, that deadline may already be uncomfortably close.
</Callout>

## What "post-quantum" does not mean

Post-quantum cryptography is not quantum cryptography. It is classical software and hardware that runs on today's machines, chosen so that no known quantum algorithm breaks it. The migration is therefore a deployment problem: protocols, certificate authorities, HSMs, firmware, libraries, procurement, and a decade of interoperability testing.

The 2022 break of SIKE is a useful antidote to complacency: a scheme that survived years of review fell to a new isogeny attack, which is why algorithm diversity and conservative choices matter. <Cite id="castryck-decru2023" locator="pp. 423–447" />

## A scenario that makes the timeline concrete

A hospital archives genomic records with a 25-year confidentiality requirement. The adversary records today's TLS sessions and waits. If a cryptographically relevant quantum computer arrives in 15 years, the data still has a decade of sensitivity left. The attack does not need to succeed today; it only needs the ciphertext and time.

This is why “we will migrate when the quantum computer exists” is not a plan. The relevant deadline is the point at which the protected data stops being sensitive, minus the time required to inventory, procure, deploy and validate the replacement. NIST discusses this timeline logic alongside its transition schedule. <Cite id="nist-ir8547" locator="§3–4" />

## Build the threat model from the asset

Start with a bounded question: which adversary can obtain which ciphertext, how long can they retain it, and what would success be worth? A passive observer may record clinical transfers cheaply and wait; a compromised backup operator may obtain ciphertext in bulk. Neither needs to break TLS today. Their incentive is the future value of the data. NIST separates cryptographic impact from operational transition because these are different risk calculations. <Cite id="nist-ir8105" locator="§4–5" />

The defender faces costly gateway, device and archive upgrades. Spend effort where confidentiality lifetime, migration lead time and retrospective-disclosure cost are all high. A short public web session and a 25-year genomic archive may use the same TLS library but have different quantum deadlines. Record those lifetimes before choosing an algorithm.

### Sensitivity is a date range, not a single label

An archive does not have one exposure deadline. If records arrive continuously, each cohort has its own creation date, retention rule and migration route. Let a record created at time $C$ need secrecy until $E$, and suppose the replacement becomes available at time $T$. An adversary who records traffic at time $C$ and can decrypt at time $B$ obtains the plaintext when $C \le B < E$. Upgrading at $T$ protects later traffic but cannot retroactively protect earlier ciphertext.

Consider a five-year collection of genomic records, each retained for ten years, with a four-year migration. Under a twelve-year quantum-arrival scenario, the first cohort stops being sensitive at year 15 and the last at year 20. Completing migration at year 4 protects traffic created afterwards, but ciphertext captured in years 0–4 remains exposed from year 12 until its own expiry: three years for the first cohort and eight years for the last. The planning question is therefore not only “when do we finish?” but “which already-recorded cohorts can we tolerate exposing, and can we re-wrap or selectively destroy them?”

This changes the inventory. Store the creation time, required sensitivity end date, storage cipher, key-wrapping key and feasible re-encryption route for each cohort. Authenticity has a different clock: a forged firmware update can cause harm immediately and can remain dangerous for the device's service life, so signature migration cannot borrow the confidentiality calculation unchanged. NIST distinguishes these use cases and stresses that past migrations have taken over a decade. <Cite id="nist-ir8547" locator="§3 and §3.1.1" />

| Asset cohort | Relevant clock | Useful action before quantum arrival |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fresh interactive traffic | Session lifetime plus future replay value | Negotiate hybrid key establishment now |
| Historical encrypted archive | Latest sensitivity date of any retained record | Prioritise re-wrapping, key rotation or verified deletion |
| Firmware signing root | Lifetime of every device that trusts it | Build a second verification path before rotating |
| Published signed manifest | Authenticity and non-repudiation policy | Plan cross-signing and timestamped re-issuance |

### Prerequisites and boundaries

You need basic familiarity with public and secret keys, encryption and signatures, plus the idea that attack cost grows exponentially with a security parameter. You do not need to simulate a quantum computer. An estimate such as $2^{64}$ describes idealised attack work, not a particular machine’s circuit depth, error rate or electricity budget.

Three misconceptions cause bad decisions. A quantum computer is not a universal decryption device: Shor exploits period and order structure; Grover exploits unstructured search. <Cite id="shor1994" locator="pp. 124–134" /> <Cite id="grover1996" locator="pp. 212–219" /> Doubling every key is not an adequate response to Shor, because the relevant problem becomes polynomial-time. Finally, post-quantum does not mean permanently safe: it means no efficient attack is known under the stated model, and cryptanalysis can change that assessment.

## The breakage map

| Primitive | Examples | Quantum impact | Migration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Factoring | RSA encryption, RSA signatures | Broken by Shor | ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA |
| Finite-field discrete log | DH, DSA | Broken by Shor | ML-KEM, ML-DSA |
| Elliptic-curve discrete log | ECDH, ECDSA, EdDSA | Broken by Shor | ML-KEM, ML-DSA |
| Symmetric encryption | AES-128/256 | Grover halves security | Prefer AES-256 |
| Hashing | SHA-256/384/512 | Grover/BHT reduce margins | Use standard PQC parameter sets |
| Code-based KEM | Classic McEliece | No known efficient quantum attack | Candidate / niche deployments |

NIST finalised the first three post-quantum standards in August 2024: ML-KEM for key encapsulation, ML-DSA for signatures, and SLH-DSA for stateless hash-based signatures. <Cite id="fips203" locator="§1" /> <Cite id="nist-pqc" locator="Selected algorithms and migration resources" /> HQC was selected in 2025 as an additional code-based KEM, and additional signature schemes remain on the standardisation track. <Cite id="nist-ir8545" locator="§3–4" />

## Security categories, not bit counts

NIST's PQC categories compare schemes to familiar symmetric targets:

- **Category 1** — roughly as hard to break as AES-128 key search.
- **Category 3** — roughly AES-192.
- **Category 5** — roughly AES-256.

These are comparison points, not proofs, and they apply to the whole scheme rather than to a single parameter. A category-3 KEM and a category-3 signature can have very different key sizes and failure modes.

### From a category label to an attack budget

A category is useful for procurement, but it is not an instruction to count all attacks in the same units. Generic quantum search, collision search and Shor's polynomial-time algorithms occupy different cost models. The table below uses idealised query counts; it deliberately excludes gate costs, parallelism, error correction and implementation constants.

| Operation | Classical generic model | Quantum generic model | Planning consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AES-128 key recovery | $2^{128}$ trial keys | About $2^{64}$ Grover iterations | Do not describe AES-128 as retaining 128 quantum bits |
| AES-256 key recovery | $2^{256}$ trial keys | About $2^{128}$ Grover iterations | A common quantum margin for long-lived bulk data |
| SHA-256 preimage | $2^{256}$ trials | About $2^{128}$ oracle calls | Hashing is weakened, not eliminated |
| SHA-256 collision | About $2^{128}$ work | About $2^{85}$ in the balanced BHT query model | Collision resistance has a smaller quantum margin than preimage resistance |
| RSA or elliptic-curve key recovery | Subexponential or exponential classical models | Polynomial-time order finding | Increasing the classical bit length is not a durable repair |

The AES values follow from Grover's search bound, not from a benchmark of a quantum AES implementation. <Cite id="grover1996" locator="pp. 212–219" /> <Cite id="fips197" locator="§4–6" /> The hash rows distinguish preimage and collision properties; NIST's impact analysis treats them separately. <Cite id="nist-ir8105" locator="§4" /> <Cite id="fips180-4" locator="§4–6" />

Two mistakes follow from reading this table too literally. First, $2^{64}$ oracle calls is not $2^{64}$ CPU cycles: each call must run a reversible cipher predicate coherently, and the complete circuit may be vastly more expensive. Second, a PQC category is not automatically obtained by halving an AES bit count. Lattice attacks are not unstructured key search, and their estimates use different algorithms and security criteria. Record the category, the best-known attack model and the date of the assessment as separate fields.

For hybrid transport, the protocol's combiner and transcript binding matter as much as either component's category. TLS 1.3 defines the key schedule into which a negotiated group's output is placed; it does not make arbitrary concatenation safe. <Cite id="rfc9846" locator="§7.1" />

## Where the transition happens first

The practical order is usually:

1. **Confidentiality-first transport** — hybrid key exchange in TLS, SSH and VPNs, because captured traffic is the immediate harvest-now risk.
2. **Signing and firmware** — code signing, certificate authorities and long-lived roots, because trust anchors outlive the hardware that uses them.
3. **Protocols with baked-in algorithms** — proprietary VPNs, smart-card protocols, industrial systems.
4. **Data-at-rest and archives** — often symmetric and less urgent, but key-wrapping layers still need review.

NIST IR 8547 (final, October 2026) deprecates quantum-vulnerable public-key cryptography after 2030 and disallows it under NIST standards and guidelines after 2035. Federal high-value and high-impact systems face earlier deadlines: post-quantum key establishment by 31 December 2030 and digital signatures by 31 December 2031. <Cite id="nist-ir8547" locator="§4 and Tables 2–5" />

Combining X25519 with ML-KEM means traffic stays secure if either component survives. It also gives the ecosystem time to find implementation bugs in the new component without betting everything on it.
</Callout>

## A hybrid handshake, field by field

Consider the standardized X25519MLKEM768 group. The client generates an ephemeral X25519 key pair and an ML-KEM-768 key pair. Its 1,216-byte key share is the 1,184-byte ML-KEM encapsulation key followed by the 32-byte X25519 public key; the reversed name order is a historical convention, not the wire order. The server&apos;s 1,120-byte share is the 1,088-byte ML-KEM ciphertext followed by its 32-byte X25519 public key. Both sides obtain a 32-byte secret from each component and form the 64-byte shared secret in the same ML-KEM-then-X25519 order. <Cite id="rfc10024" locator="§4.1–4.3" /> The sizes are fixed by the selected group and parameter set, not guessed from the name. <Cite id="fips203" locator="Table 3" /> NIST’s final transition guidance supports hybrid deployment as a hedge against cryptanalytic or implementation failure, while stressing that hybrids add complexity and are usually temporary. <Cite id="nist-ir8547" locator="§3.2–3.2.1" />

The transcript and key schedule are protocol-defined. RFC 9954&apos;s TLS construction concatenates fixed-length component secrets in the group-defined order and passes the result into the TLS key schedule; the security argument depends on that exact encoding and transcript binding. Do not invent a different order, add ambiguous length fields, or bypass the labelled HKDF schedule. <Cite id="hybrid-design" locator="§3.2–3.3 and §6" /> A server must not silently select the classical half because a large ClientHello was inconvenient; that lets an attacker steer clients away from PQC. Rollback is a controlled action, not a negotiation outcome.

ML-KEM-768 is a common general-purpose balance; ML-KEM-1024 costs more bandwidth but targets a higher category. <Cite id="fips203" locator="Table 3" /> Use a hybrid when at least one component is independently trustworthy and the protocol specifies a sound combiner. PQC-only may suit a jointly upgraded closed fleet. Classical fallback needs an explicit retirement date, owner and telemetry signal.

## Shor: from period finding to factoring

Shor's algorithm reduces factoring $N$ and discrete logarithms in a group $G$ to order finding. For factoring, choose $a$ coprime to $N$ and find the multiplicative order $r$ of $a \bmod N$; with sufficient probability, $\gcd(a^{r/2} \pm 1, N)$ yields a non-trivial factor. The quantum subroutine uses phase estimation on the unitary

$$
U_a \ket{x} = \ket{a x \bmod N}
$$

to recover the period with polynomial precision. The end-to-end circuit is polynomial in $\log N$. <Cite id="shor1994" locator="pp. 124–134" />

The consequence is structural: for RSA and elliptic curves there is no parameter increase that restores security against a large enough quantum computer. The relevant question is engineering scale and error correction, not asymptotic hardness.

## Grover: a square-root, not a break

Grover's algorithm finds a marked item in a space of size $N$ using $O(\sqrt{N})$ oracle calls. For a $k$-bit key, exhaustive search drops from $2^k$ to roughly $2^{k/2}$ operations, with substantial constant factors and the need for long coherent computation. <Cite id="grover1996" locator="pp. 212–219" />

For collision search, the BHT algorithm and its variants give a quantum collision cost around $2^{n/3}$ for an $n$-bit hash, which is why hash-based signature parameter sets are chosen with quantum margins in mind.
$$
\text{classical preimage} \approx 2^n, \qquad
\text{Grover preimage} \approx 2^{n/2}.
$$

## Shor's hidden engineering parameters

For factoring, order finding does not return a factor directly. It returns the period $r$ of $x \mapsto a^x \bmod N$. Factoring succeeds when $r$ is even and $a^{r/2} \not\equiv \pm1 \pmod N$; the two gcd calculations then reveal non-trivial factors. For $N=15$ and $a=7$, the period is $4$, because $7^2 \equiv4 \pmod{15}$, and $\gcd(4-1,15)=3$ while $\gcd(4+1,15)=5$. This checks the algebra, not the resources needed for RSA. <Cite id="shor1994" locator="pp. 124–134" />

Let $n=\lceil\log_2 N\rceil$. A straightforward reversible implementation repeatedly performs modular exponentiation using schoolbook modular multiplication. Multiplication of two $n$-bit integers uses $O(n^2)$ elementary reversible operations, and exponentiation invokes it $O(n)$ times, giving $O(n^3)$ operations before the phase-estimation circuit, continued-fraction processing and error-correction overhead. That is an implementation model, not a universal lower bound: optimized arithmetic changes the constants and the trade between qubits and depth, while Shor's structural conclusion remains polynomial time.

### Anatomy of a resource estimate

“Polynomial time” does not say whether an attack fits on a machine. A useful estimate separates quantities that are often conflated:

| Quantity | What it measures | Why changing it changes the answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical qubits | Qubits after error correction | Arithmetic workspace, QFT registers and factories all contribute |
| Logical operations | Gate count at the error-corrected level | Toffoli counts and rotation synthesis are not interchangeable |
| Circuit depth | Longest dependency chain | Deep circuits need long coherence; wide circuits need more hardware |
| Physical error model | Gate, measurement, idle and correlated errors | Determines code distance and logical failure probability |
| Code cycle time | Time for one error-correction round | Converts logical depth into elapsed time |
| Reaction time | Time to detect and correct a fault | Slower correction requires more redundancy |
| Factories and routing | Magic-state supply and qubit movement | Can dominate area and wall-clock time despite a small core circuit |

A schematic footprint is logical workspace multiplied by the spatial cost of the chosen code, plus routing and factory area. A schematic runtime is logical depth multiplied by code-cycle time, plus refresh, factory and classical-control overhead. Neither formula is a prediction until the code, hardware, arithmetic, parallelism and success probability are fixed. Two estimates are comparable only when they state those assumptions and count the same end-to-end task.

A concrete example shows why the assumptions belong next to the headline. Gidney and Ekerå estimate that factoring RSA-2048 could take eight hours using 20 million noisy physical qubits, assuming a planar nearest-neighbour superconducting architecture, physical gate error rate $10^{-3}$, a one-microsecond surface-code cycle and a ten-microsecond reaction time. Their construction accounts for noise, repeated attempts and spacetime layout. In the abstract circuit model it uses $3n+0.002n\log_2 n$ logical qubits and $0.3n^3+0.0005n^3\log_2 n$ Toffoli gates for an $n$-bit modulus. These are conditional engineering estimates, not a demonstration that such a machine exists. <Cite id="gidney-ekera2021" locator="Vol. 5, Article 433, abstract and §2–4" />

Elliptic-curve estimates expose a different tradeoff. Roetteler and colleagues give an abstract-circuit bound of $9n+2\lceil\log_2 n\rceil+10$ qubits and $448n^3\log_2 n+4090n^3$ Toffoli gates for discrete logarithms over an $n$-bit prime field. That is a gate-level resource model, not a physical-qubit count or wall-clock forecast. Comparing it directly with Gidney–Ekerå's 20 million physical qubits would compare unlike quantities. <Cite id="roetteler2017" locator="LNCS 10625, pp. 241–270" />

This is why an estimate is evidence with an expiry date, not an arrival announcement. A smaller logical circuit can still be slower if it requires a deeper computation; a faster clock can be negated by worse error rates; and a low logical-gate count can hide expensive non-Clifford factories. Keep the estimate's source, target modulus, success probability, error model, cycle time and parallelism alongside any date derived from it.

### What a resource estimate can and cannot settle

An estimate can compare architectures under explicit assumptions and show which bottleneck matters most. It cannot establish a firm arrival date, because physical error rates, fabrication yield, routing, compiler quality and factory throughput are engineering forecasts rather than cryptographic constants. Algorithmic improvements can also change the circuit after publication. Treat a headline date as the output of a scenario, not as a measurement.

Shor's discrete-logarithm reduction uses the same order-finding idea in a cyclic group, but the group-operation circuit differs between a finite field and an elliptic curve. Polynomial asymptotic complexity applies to both; gate cost, qubit layout and available parallelism do not transfer unchanged. This is another reason to keep “broken in principle” separate from “breakable by this machine by this date.” <Cite id="shor1994" locator="pp. 124–134" />

For migration decisions, use ranges and triggers. A range might combine an early architectural scenario, a central estimate and a delayed-correction scenario. Triggers include a published circuit improvement, a demonstrated logical-gate milestone or a standards-policy deadline. Each trigger has an owner and a pre-agreed action, such as accelerating a pilot or retiring a fallback. This preserves the value of technical forecasts without making the security programme depend on predicting one exact year.

## Grover as a bounded coherent search

Suppose a search space has size $N$ and $M$ marked items. Set $\theta=\arcsin\sqrt{M/N}$. Starting from the standard uniform superposition, the probability of observing a marked item after $j$ full Grover iterations is $\sin^2((2j+1)\theta)$. For one marked item, the first maximum is near $\pi/4$ iterations; the cost is $O(\sqrt{N/M})$, not a promise that any fixed iteration count succeeds with certainty. <Cite id="grover1996" locator="pp. 212–219" />

For key recovery, the oracle must coherently test a candidate key against a known plaintext-ciphertext relation. The query count is only the outer loop. A resource model must also count the reversible AES circuit in each query, the ancillas it needs, the uncomputation that restores workspace, and whether independent oracle evaluations can be parallelised. Trading more physical qubits for less depth can reduce runtime without reducing total gate work; quoting only the minimum of either number overstates what a machine can do.

## Collision search needs a memory model

For an ideal random function with an $n$-bit output, the BHT family trades quantum oracle queries against stored quantum data. In its balanced regime, query time $q$ and memory $r$ satisfy the heuristic relation

$$
q^2 r \gtrsim 2^n,
$$

with the standard BHT operating point $q \approx r \approx 2^{n/3}$. For SHA-256 this gives the familiar query-model scale $2^{256/3}\approx2^{85.3}$. It is not a universal lower bound for every structured hash or every implementation, and memory retrieval is not free. It does explain why NIST treats collision resistance as a distinct quantum risk. <Cite id="brassard-hoyer-tapp1998" locator="LNCS 1380, pp. 163–169" /> <Cite id="nist-ir8105" locator="§4" />

| Cost model | What a number means | What it omits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oracle queries | Calls to an idealised primitive | Cipher/hash circuit cost and workspace |
| Logical gates | Error-corrected computational work | Physical code overhead and routing |
| Circuit depth | Sequential dependency length | Available parallelism and factories |
| Wall-clock estimate | Time under stated hardware assumptions | Forecast uncertainty and future optimisation |

The security conclusion survives these omissions: Shor attacks the structure of factoring and discrete logarithms, whereas Grover and BHT change generic search margins. The omissions matter when deciding whether an attack is feasible on a particular machine, which is why migration policy should not wait for a single headline resource number.


## What a security category means
NIST categories are calibrated against the cost of key search on AES. They are not theorems about a particular attack circuit. A scheme is placed in a category because the best known attacks — classical and quantum — are at least as expensive as the corresponding symmetric target, under stated assumptions.

<Callout variant="lab" title="Reduction sketch">
For lattice KEMs, the chain is roughly: break IND-CCA2 of the KEM $\Rightarrow$ break the FO-wrapped PKE $\Rightarrow$ solve Module-LWE (or a related problem) with non-negligible advantage. Each arrow carries a loss factor; the parameters are chosen so the loss does not collapse the advantage. The proof does not say "quantum computers cannot solve lattices"; it says the scheme is as hard as the underlying problem, and that problem has no known efficient quantum algorithm.
</Callout>

## The honest uncertainty

We do not know when a cryptographically relevant quantum computer will exist, and we do not have a proof that lattices are hard for quantum computers. What we do have is a large body of cryptanalysis, multiple independent families, and a standardisation process designed to avoid a single point of failure. Planning under uncertainty is exactly what hybrid deployment and crypto agility are for.

## The migration is a systems problem

A PQC transition touches more than a library call:

- **Wire formats** change. ML-KEM-768 encapsulation keys are 1,184 bytes and ciphertexts 1,088 bytes; ML-DSA-65 signatures are 3,309 bytes. Certificate chains and handshake flights grow.
- **Trust anchors** live for decades. A root CA that signs with a quantum-vulnerable algorithm today may still be trusted when the quantum computer arrives.
- **Embedded devices** cannot always be updated. Firmware signing keys, secure boot roots and long-lived IoT credentials need a plan for cryptographic agility.
- **Side channels** remain. A new algorithm does not fix a secret-dependent branch or a leaky table lookup.

## Hybrid key exchange in practice

A hybrid group such as X25519MLKEM768 combines an ephemeral X25519 share with an ML-KEM-768 encapsulation. For this standardized group, both the key shares and the 64-byte shared secret place ML-KEM first and X25519 second; other hybrid groups can choose another order. The combined secret is fed into the TLS 1.3 key schedule. <Cite id="rfc10024" locator="§4.1–4.3" /> The combiner is designed to preserve security if either component is secure, while the classical component preserves compatibility during rollout. <Cite id="rfc9846" locator="§7.1" /> NIST’s final transition guidance supports hybrid deployment as a hedge against cryptanalytic or implementation failure, while stressing that hybrids add complexity and are usually temporary. <Cite id="nist-ir8547" locator="§3.2–3.2.1" />

<Callout variant="warning" title="Size is a protocol parameter">
A 1.2 KB key share and a 1.1 KB ciphertext can push a ClientHello past common buffer assumptions. Test fragmentation, middlebox behaviour, record limits and HSM command sizes before turning hybrid groups on by default.
</Callout>
## Diversity is a security control

The SIKE attack is the canonical lesson: a scheme can be mathematically elegant, heavily reviewed, and still fall. <Cite id="castryck-decru2023" locator="pp. 423–447" /> Algorithm diversity — lattices, hashes, codes — limits the blast radius of a single cryptanalytic advance. Classic McEliece remains attractive in niches where its megabyte-scale public keys are acceptable, precisely because its assumptions are old and conservative. <Cite id="mceliece-spec" locator="§2–3" />

## Implementation patterns and performance constraints

Use a narrow provider interface that accepts an algorithm identifier, parameter set and operation context. Keep wire encoders, key storage and trust policy outside the mathematical routine. Version every stored key and certificate envelope, and make unknown identifiers a policy decision. Crypto agility is real only if a second implementation can be tested and selected.

Budget bytes, CPU or coprocessor time, peak memory, and key-state persistence. A KEM can be fast yet fail a constrained device because its stack is too large. A signature can be cheap to generate yet overwhelm concurrent verifiers. Record p50 and p99 latency, allocation peaks and failure rates by parameter set, including the slowest supported device.

New arithmetic creates side-channel surfaces: secret-dependent lookups, variable-time division, early malformed-input rejection and distinguishable success paths. FIPS 203 makes constant-time decapsulation and rejection behaviour implementation requirements, so functional tests are insufficient. <Cite id="fips203" locator="§3.3" /> Run vendor vectors, cross-implementation tests, fuzzing and fault injection. Negative hybrid tests should corrupt one component, block one implementation and attempt an unapproved downgrade.

Maintain two trust hierarchies during transition. Exercise the new PQC root before it is required; retain old roots only for tracked legacy paths. Rehearse issuance, revocation, offline signing and recovery with larger objects. Instrument negotiated groups so retirement follows measured adoption. Standardisation defines interfaces, but not ownership, change windows or recovery plans. <Cite id="nist-pqc" locator="Selected algorithms and migration resources" />


## Schedule evidence gates, not a single switch-over date

A migration duration is a distribution, not a constant. Break it into inventory, protocol design, procurement, interoperability, staged rollout and retirement. The critical path is often the slowest vendor or least replaceable device, while the earliest deadline belongs to the longest-lived asset. Record both a latest completion date and the confidence interval for each phase.

Suppose a programme needs six years and a particular trust anchor remains sensitive for twenty-five years. A ten-year arrival scenario leaves only four years to start; fifteen years leaves nine; twenty years leaves fourteen. Under the earliest scenario, even starting now consumes most of the margin. These are not forecasts. They are stress tests that reveal which procurement or certification delays would force a risk-acceptance decision.

| Gate | Evidence required | Decision it supports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inventory complete | Algorithm, parameter set, owner, data lifetime and update path recorded | Which assets cannot wait for a perfect forecast |
| Protocol pilot | Production-size handshakes, fragmentation and downgrade tests pass | Whether the target framing is deployable |
| Trust transition | New root issued, overlap tested and recovery rehearsed | When old roots can stop signing |
| Fleet rollout | Coverage by hardware generation, not just endpoint count | Which cohorts may retire classical-only mode |
| Retirement | Fallback disabled by policy and telemetry confirms adoption | Whether migration debt is actually closed |

Keep quantum-arrival assumptions in a versioned risk register. An updated resource estimate should trigger a recalculation of deadlines, not an automatic panic switch. Conversely, a slower estimate should not restore a retired classical path: procurement, interoperability and incident recovery all have memories that a spreadsheet forecast does not.

Rollback needs the same discipline. A rollback bundle should restore a complete, compatible set of library, key encoding, certificate and trust policy. It should not silently re-enable classical-only negotiation or resurrect a retired root. Exercise failure of the PQC component, malformed large messages and loss of a new trust anchor separately. The useful outcome is a controlled return to an approved state with an alert, not an attacker-influenced downgrade.

## A migration runbook

1. **Inventory** algorithms, protocols, certificates, firmware, HSMs and third-party dependencies. Include key lifetimes, not just endpoints.
2. **Classify** each use by confidentiality lifetime, authenticity lifetime, performance constraints and update ability.
3. **Choose** a hybrid or PQC-only target per protocol; record why.
4. **Measure** handshake sizes, CPU cost, memory, fragmentation and hardware support.
5. **Pilot** with telemetry and rollback; test negative paths and downgrade resistance.
6. **Retire** classical-only configurations on a documented schedule, not when an incident forces it.
7. **Exercise** the replacement of a root key, a firmware-signing key and a protocol group.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Do not forget the boring systems">
Certificate authorities, code-signing infrastructure, backups and embedded devices are often the long pole. A modern TLS stack does not help if the firmware verification key cannot be rotated.
</Callout>
</Lens>


## Common failure modes

| Failure mode | Why it happens | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deadline tied to hardware arrival | Harvest risk is confused with present attack feasibility | Long-lived ciphertext remains exposed |
| Silent classical fallback | Large PQC messages are dropped | Attacker steers peers away from PQC |
| Fixed 2 KB buffers | Parsers assume classical object sizes | Handshake or certificate failure |
| Uninventoried trust anchors | Firmware and offline roots are missed | Vulnerable roots survive endpoint upgrades |
| Unversioned identifiers | Old and new encodings share a field | Ambiguous keys and trust decisions |
| One-family portfolio | Every system shares one assumption | One advance has system-wide impact |
| Success-only testing | Component failure is never exercised | Recovery paths fail in incidents |
| PQC label without parameters | Category and encoding are omitted | Weak or incompatible sets enter production |

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hybrid fails behind a load balancer | Extension is stripped or truncated | Capture both sides and update the parser |
| Weak clients always fall back | Fallback is automatic | Restrict by cohort and alert on downgrades |
| Decapsulation failures spike | Length, endian or certificate mismatch | Compare encodings with standard vectors |
| HSM rejects a valid key | Object exceeds the command buffer | Use a supported format or smaller parameter set |
| New certificates fail in the fleet | Trust-store support is inconsistent | Pilot a second root and stage distribution |
| Small devices slow down | Allocation, NTT backend or entropy source is unsuitable | Profile resources and test the worst device |

## Design review checklist

- [ ] Every key has a confidentiality or authenticity lifetime and owner.
- [ ] Migration start uses a range of quantum-arrival assumptions.
- [ ] RSA, DH, ECDH, DSA, ECDSA and EdDSA are inventoried, including firmware.
- [ ] Protocol identifiers, parameter sets and canonical encodings are recorded.
- [ ] Hybrid composition uses a specified KDF and transcript binding.
- [ ] Fallback is authenticated, observable and scheduled for retirement.
- [ ] Maximum handshake, certificate and HSM command sizes are tested end to end.
- [ ] Critical trust anchors have an independent family or provider.
- [ ] Randomness, malformed input and component failures have exercised recovery.
- [ ] Telemetry records negotiated algorithms, sizes, latency and rollbacks.
- [ ] Rotation, revocation and emergency recovery have been rehearsed.
- [ ] Classical-only debt has a funded owner and deadline.

## Key takeaways

- Shor breaks factoring and discrete log; Grover changes symmetric margins.
- The migration deadline is driven by data lifetime and migration time, not by a single predicted date.
- Hybrid key exchange protects traffic while the PQC ecosystem matures.
- Algorithm diversity and crypto agility are risk controls, not buzzwords.
- Inventory and key lifetime matter more than a slide claiming “quantum-safe”.
