## The short version

Two quantum algorithms change the security landscape, and they do not do the same thing.

- **Shor's algorithm** solves integer factorisation and discrete logarithms efficiently. It does not weaken RSA or elliptic-curve cryptography; it removes their underlying hardness assumption entirely. <Cite id="shor1994" locator="pp. 124–134" />
- **Grover's algorithm** gives a quadratic speed-up for unstructured search. It halves the effective security level of symmetric keys and hash preimages, but it does not break AES or SHA-2 outright. <Cite id="grover1996" locator="pp. 212–219" />

That distinction drives everything that follows. RSA, finite-field Diffie–Hellman, ECDH, ECDSA and EdDSA need replacement. AES and SHA need larger parameters, not a new design. NIST's 2016 report already framed the problem this way. <Cite id="nist-ir8105" locator="§4–5" />

## The attacker you should actually worry about

A cryptographically relevant quantum computer does not need to exist today for the threat to be real. An adversary can record encrypted traffic now and decrypt it later. For data with a long confidentiality lifetime — health records, source code, diplomatic cables, firmware signing keys — "later" is inside the risk window.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Harvest now, decrypt later">
The migration deadline is not the date a quantum computer appears. It is the date when the data you protected today is still sensitive, minus the time it takes to migrate. For a 25-year secret, that deadline may already be uncomfortably close.
</Callout>

## What "post-quantum" does not mean

Post-quantum cryptography is not quantum cryptography. It is classical software and hardware that runs on today's machines, chosen so that no known quantum algorithm breaks it. The migration is therefore a deployment problem: protocols, certificate authorities, HSMs, firmware, libraries, procurement, and a decade of interoperability testing.

The 2022 break of SIKE is a useful antidote to complacency: a scheme that survived years of review fell to a new isogeny attack, which is why algorithm diversity and conservative choices matter. <Cite id="castryck-decru2023" locator="pp. 423–447" />

## A scenario that makes the timeline concrete

A hospital archives genomic records with a 25-year confidentiality requirement. The adversary records today's TLS sessions and waits. If a cryptographically relevant quantum computer arrives in 15 years, the data still has a decade of sensitivity left. The attack does not need to succeed today; it only needs the ciphertext and time.

This is why “we will migrate when the quantum computer exists” is not a plan. The relevant deadline is the point at which the protected data stops being sensitive, minus the time required to inventory, procure, deploy and validate the replacement. <Cite id="nist-ir8547" locator="§4" />
</Lens>

## The breakage map

| Primitive | Examples | Quantum impact | Migration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Factoring | RSA encryption, RSA signatures | Broken by Shor | ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA |
| Finite-field discrete log | DH, DSA | Broken by Shor | ML-KEM, ML-DSA |
| Elliptic-curve discrete log | ECDH, ECDSA, EdDSA | Broken by Shor | ML-KEM, ML-DSA |
| Symmetric encryption | AES-128/256 | Grover halves security | Prefer AES-256 |
| Hashing | SHA-256/384/512 | Grover/BHT reduce margins | Use standard PQC parameter sets |
| Code-based KEM | Classic McEliece | No known efficient quantum attack | Candidate / niche deployments |

NIST finalised the first three post-quantum standards in August 2024: ML-KEM for key encapsulation, ML-DSA for signatures, and SLH-DSA for stateless hash-based signatures. <Cite id="fips203" locator="§1" /> <Cite id="nist-pqc" /> HQC was selected in 2025 as an additional code-based KEM, and additional signature schemes remain on the standardisation track. <Cite id="nist-ir8545" locator="§3–4" />

## Security categories, not bit counts

NIST's PQC categories compare schemes to familiar symmetric targets:

- **Category 1** — roughly as hard to break as AES-128 key search.
- **Category 3** — roughly AES-192.
- **Category 5** — roughly AES-256.

These are comparison points, not proofs, and they apply to the whole scheme rather than to a single parameter. A category-3 KEM and a category-3 signature can have very different key sizes and failure modes.

## Where the transition happens first

The practical order is usually:

1. **Confidentiality-first transport** — hybrid key exchange in TLS, SSH and VPNs, because captured traffic is the immediate harvest-now risk.
2. **Signing and firmware** — code signing, certificate authorities and long-lived roots, because trust anchors outlive the hardware that uses them.
3. **Protocols with baked-in algorithms** — proprietary VPNs, smart-card protocols, industrial systems.
4. **Data-at-rest and archives** — often symmetric and less urgent, but key-wrapping layers still need review.

NIST IR 8547 sketches a transition in which 112-bit classical algorithms are deprecated after 2030 and disallowed after 2035 for federal use; it is a draft proposal, but a useful planning signal. <Cite id="nist-ir8547" locator="§4–5" />

<Callout variant="note" title="Hybrid is the default, not a compromise">
Combining X25519 with ML-KEM means traffic stays secure if either component survives. It also gives the ecosystem time to find implementation bugs in the new component without betting everything on it.
</Callout>



## Worked migration order

A practical first pass looks like this:

| Priority | Asset | Why first | Typical action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Public TLS/VPN traffic | Harvest-now risk | Hybrid X25519 + ML-KEM |
| 2 | Long-lived signing keys | Trust anchors outlive hardware | Plan ML-DSA/SLH-DSA roots |
| 3 | Firmware and code signing | Devices may be unpatchable | Crypto-agile boot chain |
| 4 | Data at rest | Key wrapping and archives | Key hierarchy review |
| 5 | Proprietary protocols | Hard-coded algorithms | Design agility now |

The order is not universal, but it forces the question “what is the confidentiality or authenticity lifetime of this asset?”
</Lens>

## Shor: from period finding to factoring

Shor's algorithm reduces factoring $N$ and discrete logarithms in a group $G$ to order finding. For factoring, choose $a$ coprime to $N$ and find the multiplicative order $r$ of $a \bmod N$; with sufficient probability, $\gcd(a^{r/2} \pm 1, N)$ yields a non-trivial factor. The quantum subroutine uses phase estimation on the unitary

$$
U_a \ket{x} = \ket{a x \bmod N}
$$

to recover the period with polynomial precision. The end-to-end circuit is polynomial in $\log N$. <Cite id="shor1994" locator="pp. 124–134" />

The consequence is structural: for RSA and elliptic curves there is no parameter increase that restores security against a large enough quantum computer. The relevant question is engineering scale and error correction, not asymptotic hardness.

## Grover: a square-root, not a break

Grover's algorithm finds a marked item in a space of size $N$ using $O(\sqrt{N})$ oracle calls. For a $k$-bit key, exhaustive search drops from $2^k$ to roughly $2^{k/2}$ operations, with substantial constant factors and the need for long coherent computation. <Cite id="grover1996" locator="pp. 212–219" />

For collision search, the BHT algorithm and its variants give a quantum collision cost around $2^{n/3}$ for an $n$-bit hash, which is why hash-based signature parameter sets are chosen with quantum margins in mind.

$$
\text{classical preimage} \approx 2^n, \qquad \text{Grover preimage} \approx 2^{n/2}.
$$

## What a security category means

NIST categories are calibrated against the cost of key search on AES. They are not theorems about a particular attack circuit. A scheme is placed in a category because the best known attacks — classical and quantum — are at least as expensive as the corresponding symmetric target, under stated assumptions.

<Callout variant="lab" title="Reduction sketch">
For lattice KEMs, the chain is roughly: break IND-CCA2 of the KEM $\Rightarrow$ break the FO-wrapped PKE $\Rightarrow$ solve Module-LWE (or a related problem) with non-negligible advantage. Each arrow carries a loss factor; the parameters are chosen so the loss does not collapse the advantage. The proof does not say "quantum computers cannot solve lattices"; it says the scheme is as hard as the underlying problem, and that problem has no known efficient quantum algorithm.
</Callout>

## The honest uncertainty

We do not know when a cryptographically relevant quantum computer will exist, and we do not have a proof that lattices are hard for quantum computers. What we do have is a large body of cryptanalysis, multiple independent families, and a standardisation process designed to avoid a single point of failure. Planning under uncertainty is exactly what hybrid deployment and crypto agility are for.



## Mosca's inequality

A useful planning heuristic is Mosca's inequality: if $X$ is the time your data must stay secret, $Y$ is the time needed to migrate, and $Z$ is the time until a quantum computer can break the cryptography, then you have a problem when

$$
X + Y > Z.
$$

The uncertainty in $Z$ is exactly why hybrid deployment and crypto agility matter. You do not need to predict $Z$ precisely to start reducing $Y$. <Cite id="nist-ir8547" locator="§4–5" />

## The migration is a systems problem

A PQC transition touches more than a library call:

- **Wire formats** change. ML-KEM-768 encapsulation keys are 1,184 bytes and ciphertexts 1,088 bytes; ML-DSA-65 signatures are 3,309 bytes. Certificate chains and handshake flights grow.
- **Trust anchors** live for decades. A root CA that signs with a quantum-vulnerable algorithm today may still be trusted when the quantum computer arrives.
- **Embedded devices** cannot always be updated. Firmware signing keys, secure boot roots and long-lived IoT credentials need a plan for cryptographic agility.
- **Side channels** remain. A new algorithm does not fix a secret-dependent branch or a leaky table lookup.

## Hybrid key exchange in practice

A hybrid group such as X25519MLKEM768 combines an ephemeral X25519 share with an ML-KEM-768 encapsulation. Both shared secrets are fed into the TLS 1.3 key schedule. The connection remains secure if either component is secure, and the classical component preserves compatibility during rollout. <Cite id="fips203" locator="§8" /> <Cite id="nist-ir8547" locator="§4" />

<Callout variant="warning" title="Size is a protocol parameter">
A 1.2 KB key share and a 1.1 KB ciphertext can push a ClientHello past common buffer assumptions. Test fragmentation, middlebox behaviour, record limits and HSM command sizes before turning hybrid groups on by default.
</Callout>

## Diversity is a security control

The SIKE attack is the canonical lesson: a scheme can be mathematically elegant, heavily reviewed, and still fall. <Cite id="castryck-decru2023" locator="pp. 423–447" /> Algorithm diversity — lattices, hashes, codes — limits the blast radius of a single cryptanalytic advance. Classic McEliece remains attractive in niches where its megabyte-scale public keys are acceptable, precisely because its assumptions are old and conservative. <Cite id="mceliece-spec" locator="§2–3" />

## A practical sequence

1. **Inventory** every use of RSA, DH, ECDH, ECDSA and EdDSA, including firmware and certificates.
2. **Prioritise** long-lived confidentiality and long-lived signing keys.
3. **Deploy hybrids** where the protocol supports them; keep classical fallbacks until interoperability is proven.
4. **Instrument** sizes, timings and failure modes; add crypto-agility tests to CI.
5. **Review** the implementation layer: constant-time code, fault checks, key zeroisation, and hardware acceleration.

## A migration runbook

1. **Inventory** algorithms, protocols, certificates, firmware, HSMs and third-party dependencies. Include key lifetimes, not just endpoints.
2. **Classify** each use by confidentiality lifetime, authenticity lifetime, performance constraints and update ability.
3. **Choose** a hybrid or PQC-only target per protocol; record why.
4. **Measure** handshake sizes, CPU cost, memory, fragmentation and hardware support.
5. **Pilot** with telemetry and rollback; test negative paths and downgrade resistance.
6. **Retire** classical-only configurations on a documented schedule, not when an incident forces it.
7. **Exercise** the replacement of a root key, a firmware-signing key and a protocol group.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Do not forget the boring systems">
Certificate authorities, code-signing infrastructure, backups and embedded devices are often the long pole. A modern TLS stack does not help if the firmware verification key cannot be rotated.
</Callout>
</Lens>


## Key takeaways

- Shor breaks factoring and discrete log; Grover changes symmetric margins.
- The migration deadline is driven by data lifetime and migration time, not by a single predicted date.
- Hybrid key exchange protects traffic while the PQC ecosystem matures.
- Algorithm diversity and crypto agility are risk controls, not buzzwords.
- Inventory and key lifetime matter more than a slide claiming “quantum-safe”.

## Practice

1. Pick one system you operate. Write its confidentiality lifetime, authenticity lifetime and update mechanism.
2. Identify the three places where a classical algorithm is embedded in firmware or hardware.
