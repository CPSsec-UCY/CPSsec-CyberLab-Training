## What a SOC does

A security operations centre watches telemetry, detects suspicious activity, investigates alerts and coordinates response. It is part emergency room, part intelligence desk and part engineering team.

The job is not “look at every alert”. It is to **turn noisy signals into defensible decisions**:

- Is this real?
- What is affected?
- What should we do now?
- What evidence must we preserve?
- How do we prevent it next time?

## Why alerts alone fail

A SIEM can generate thousands of alerts per day. Without context, prioritisation and playbooks, analysts drown. Burnout and alert fatigue are security failures, not HR problems.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Detection is a product">
A detection is not finished when the rule fires. It needs an owner, a severity model, a runbook, a test, a false-positive process and a metric. Otherwise it is technical debt with a pager.
</Callout>

## A day in the queue

An analyst starts with 180 open alerts. Most are low-severity scanner noise, a few are identity anomalies, one is an endpoint detection with a suspicious parent process. The analyst must decide where to spend the next hour. That decision depends on asset criticality, identity context, threat intelligence and the quality of the detection — not on the number of alerts alone.

A good SOC makes this decision cheaper: it enriches automatically, groups related alerts into cases, documents playbooks and removes rules that waste attention. A bad SOC measures success by closed tickets.

### Threat model, scenario and incentives

Treat the SOC's inputs as potentially incomplete or hostile. An attacker can delete local logs, poison timestamps, generate decoy alerts or use valid credentials to make malicious activity look routine. An insider can abuse monitoring access or tamper with evidence. A vendor integration can fail silently. The SOC must protect availability of detection, confidentiality of collected telemetry and integrity of evidence and response actions. These objectives can conflict: aggressive automatic containment protects systems but can interrupt essential services.

Consider a manufacturer with 4,000 endpoints and a central identity provider. An attacker compromises a finance account, signs in from a new device, accesses a file share and attempts lateral movement. The SOC sees identity, endpoint and network events with different clocks and confidence. The attacker wants to stay below the alert threshold and make containment expensive; a mistake that disables the finance director during month-end creates business pressure to ignore the next alert. The defender needs a fast, evidence-based decision and an authorised rollback path, not simply the highest-severity ticket.

Analysts are also threatened by volume. Each low-value alert consumes attention that could find a rare high-impact incident. Detection owners are rewarded for coverage, while responders are punished for outages; without shared metrics, this creates incentives to add rules and block cautiously. Align detection owners, responders and asset owners on precision, missed-incident cost and recovery time. Track reopened cases and containment failures alongside closure speed.

Three misconceptions weaken operations. **An alert is not an incident**: it is a hypothesis generated by a rule. **Mean time to respond is not response quality**: closing tickets quickly can hide delayed containment. **More telemetry is not automatically more visibility**: unowned, unparsed and unretained data can increase cost without supporting a decision. A SOC should be able to explain which hypothesis each detection tests, what evidence would refute it and which action the evidence justifies.

## Severity, priority and decision rights

Severity describes the potential impact if a hypothesis is true. Priority describes what the team should do next given confidence, asset criticality, exposure, active harm and available responders. Conflating them lets noisy rules consume the queue while safety-critical alerts wait. A severity label belongs to the detection and its potential consequence; a priority changes as triage adds or removes evidence.

| Operational object | Question answered | Typical owner | Closure evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Event | What did a source record? | Source or pipeline owner | Raw event retained with schema and provenance |
| Alert | Which rule hypothesis fired? | Detection owner | Rule version, query, matching fields and test context |
| Case | What story links related alerts? | Assigned analyst | Timeline, scope, hypotheses and evidence gaps |
| Incident | Has a risk-bearing event been established? | Incident lead | Confirmed impact, response record and recovery criteria |
| Action | What changed in the environment? | Authorised responder | Actor, approval, timestamp and observed outcome |

Decision rights should be explicit before the incident. A tier-one analyst may enrich, request endpoint evidence and apply a time-limited low-risk suppression. Isolating a shared service, disabling a privileged account or notifying regulators usually needs an incident lead and the relevant asset, legal or communications owner. The SOC supplies evidence and a recommendation; it does not silently acquire the authority to interrupt payroll, clinical care or manufacturing because a rule label says “critical”. NIST's incident-response guidance integrates preparation, analysis, containment, eradication, recovery and learning with risk management, which requires those organisational decisions as well as technical steps. <Cite id="sp800-61r3" locator="§2–5" />

Consider an alert for a finance account signing in from a new country and opening a payroll share. The rule's potential severity is high, but the immediate priority depends on whether downloads are continuing, whether the device is managed, whether endpoint telemetry is fresh and whether a documented payroll test positively matches the activity. If active data access continues, emergency session revocation may be justified while the business owner prepares a fallback. If the evidence is stale and the change ticket is ambiguous, the justified action is accelerated investigation and preservation—not account disablement presented as “triage complete”.

Write the handoff contract as facts, hypotheses, unknowns and requested authority. For example: “Identity and file-audit events confirm access; endpoint telemetry is 25 minutes stale; the claimed test does not name this device; active downloads are unknown; request approval to revoke sessions and isolate the laptop.” That is defensible because another analyst can reproduce the reasoning and an incident commander can approve or reject the action.

## Detection as code: one worked example

Suppose the hypothesis is “a workstation authenticated with a valid account and then spawned an encoded PowerShell process shortly after”. The detection needs:

1. Identity telemetry: successful interactive logon, source asset, user, time.
2. Endpoint telemetry: process creation, parent-child relationship, command line.
3. Correlation window: a few minutes, tuned to the environment.
4. Suppression: known administrative scripts and signed automation.
5. Response: isolate the host, capture memory, revoke sessions, hunt for lateral movement.

The rule should be versioned, tested and owned. A rule without a runbook is an alert generator, not a detection product. Sigma is useful because it separates the detection logic from a specific SIEM query language. <Cite id="sigma" locator="Rule format and conversion tooling" />

## Runbook anatomy

A usable runbook contains:

1. **Trigger:** the detection and its confidence.
2. **Context:** asset owner, business criticality and identity.
3. **Validation:** queries and evidence that confirm or refute the hypothesis.
4. **Containment:** exact actions, permissions and rollback.
5. **Escalation:** who is called, when, and with what evidence.
6. **Closure:** what to document and which detection to tune.

A runbook that says “investigate” is not a runbook. The analyst should know the next command and the decision criteria.

### Operate the telemetry pipeline

At collection, record producer, schema version and reliability. Identity, cloud and endpoint sources may delay, deduplicate or rate-limit events. Preserve event and ingestion time, and distinguish users, service accounts, devices and cloud roles during normalisation. Enrich with asset owner, criticality and entitlement, but give external lookups timeouts and cached fallbacks.

Detection rules need explicit windows and deduplication keys. A case groups related alerts into an investigable story; triage records facts, hypotheses and missing evidence. Response actions have separate audit events with actor, approval, timestamp and outcome. Containment does not complete recovery or learning.

## A detection is a data contract

A correlation rule is only as reliable as the fields and time semantics it consumes. The contract should name each source, the normalised field, its meaning, expected latency, allowed nulls, clock source and behaviour on duplicates. A user identifier is not interchangeable with an account name; a host name can be reused; a process GUID can distinguish two executions that share a command line. Preserve the original event identifier so that deduplication does not delete the only provenance. Syslog's structured format provides timestamp and structured-data mechanisms, but the pipeline must still distinguish the producer's event time from collector ingestion time. <Cite id="rfc5424" locator="§6 (timestamp, hostname and structured data)" />

| Contract element | Example decision | Failure if omitted |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entity identity | Join on immutable device ID plus user ID | Same hostname or reused account merges unrelated activity |
| Event-time window | Five minutes bounded by source event time | Ingestion delay splits or fabricates a sequence |
| Late-data policy | Emit partial case, then amend with provenance | Late endpoint evidence is silently lost or never reviewed |
| Deduplication key | Source plus stable event ID | Retries inflate counts and alert volume |
| Missing-field policy | Mark visibility gap and request backfill | Absence is interpreted as normal behaviour |

## Precision matters more than accuracy

For a detection with true-positive rate $p$, false-positive rate $q$ and attack prevalence $\pi$, the probability an alert is real is

$$
\Pr(\text{attack} \mid \text{alert}) = \frac{\pi p}{\pi p + (1-\pi)q}.
$$

When attacks are rare, even a small $q$ can make most alerts false. This is why context and base rates are central to detection engineering. NIST's IDPS guidance stresses tuning and operational integration. <Cite id="sp800-94" locator="§2–4" />

## Coverage versus noise

A detection portfolio should balance:

- **coverage** of relevant techniques,
- **precision** in the deployed environment,
- **response cost**,
- **time to detect and respond**,
- **false-negative risk**.

Adding rules increases coverage and noise. Retiring rules is part of engineering, not failure.

## Incident response under uncertainty

Incident response is decision-making with incomplete evidence. Good practice:

- preserve volatile evidence before containment when possible,
- separate confirmed facts from hypotheses,
- define containment criteria in advance,
- communicate with asset owners and legal/privacy teams,
- document every action and timestamp.

NIST SP 800-61r3 integrates response with cybersecurity risk management rather than treating it as a standalone checklist. <Cite id="sp800-61r3" locator="§2–5" />

## Detection quality under base rates

For a detection with true-positive rate $p$, false-positive rate $q$ and attack prevalence $\pi$:

$$
\Pr(\text{attack} \mid \text{alert}) = \frac{\pi p}{\pi p + (1-\pi)q}.
$$

When attacks are rare, precision can be low even at high accuracy. This is why tuning, asset context and response cost are part of detection engineering. A rule that fires on every failed login is not a control; it is a denial-of-service attack on the analyst team.

## Response under uncertainty

Incident response is decision-making with incomplete evidence. Separate confirmed facts from hypotheses, preserve volatile evidence before containment when possible, and define containment thresholds in advance. A false positive that isolates a domain controller is an outage; a delayed containment that lets ransomware spread is a breach. The playbook should make both failure modes explicit.

## Evidence preservation

Before containment, capture volatile evidence where feasible: memory, running processes, network connections, authentication tokens and relevant logs. Record hashes, timestamps and the identity of the person who collected each artefact. Containment actions can destroy evidence, so the playbook should state what to preserve and how.

Chain of custody matters for legal, regulatory and HR processes. Store evidence separately from the affected host, restrict access and document every transfer. A perfect technical response with unusable evidence can still fail the organisation's obligations.

### Formalise triage as an expected-cost decision

Let $A$ mean an incident, $D$ mean an alert and $R$ mean a response action. Precision is $\Pr(A\mid D)$ as defined above. The expected number of true and false alerts per day is $N\pi p$ and $N(1-\pi)q$, where $N$ is the daily event volume that reaches the rule. Correlation changes $N$ and prevalence at the case level, so case precision must be measured separately from alert precision. <Cite id="sp800-94" locator="§2–4" />

A response threshold should reflect asymmetric costs. If the cost of missing an incident is $C_{FN}$, the cost of investigating a false alert is $C_{FP}$ and the cost of an unnecessary disruptive action is $C_{FA}$, the analyst compares expected losses rather than severity labels alone. A high-precision signal on a critical asset may justify automatic isolation; the same precision on a shared clinical system may require human approval. This does not eliminate judgement, but it makes the decision policy explicit and testable.

The Bayesian model assumes a representative prevalence and stable detector performance. A new phishing campaign, a logging outage or a change in attacker behaviour invalidates those estimates. Alerts are correlated, so treating them as independent can overstate certainty. Identity context can be stale or spoofed. Use confidence intervals for small samples, track precision by rule and asset class, and prefer a range of plausible interpretations over false numerical certainty.

### Evidence integrity and response safety

Evidence integrity requires authenticity and continuity. Link artefacts to sources with collection metadata, hashes and access-controlled storage. Record every transfer and transformation. Hash after acquisition, store the digest separately and record collector, tool version and timestamp. A hash proves stability since hashing, not that acquisition was complete or lawful. <Cite id="sp800-92" locator="§3–5" />

Response changes the system: isolation drops connections, process termination discards memory and account disablement destroys sessions. Default to preserving feasible volatile evidence, containing harm, acquiring durable artefacts and eradicating. When active damage outweighs evidence loss, contain first and document why.

Automation is a decision function with guardrails. Require explicit authorisation, a bounded scope, idempotency, a timeout, dry-run support and a reversal procedure. Log both requested and observed outcomes. Rate-limit repeated actions to prevent a faulty enrichment source from isolating hundreds of hosts. Test with false positives and unavailable dependencies; a playbook that works only when the asset database, identity service and ticket system are healthy is not a reliable control.

NIST's incident lifecycle includes preparation, detection and analysis, containment, eradication, recovery and post-incident learning. <Cite id="sp800-61r3" locator="§2–5" /> Recovery is verified against known-good behaviour, not merely service restart. Lessons must become owned changes to detections, controls, runbooks or architecture; otherwise the same incident recurs with a new ticket number.

## Build an evidence ledger and an incident timeline

Triage should leave two linked products: an evidence ledger that says what is known and how, and a timeline that says when it happened and what the SOC did. The forensic process distinguishes collection, examination, analysis and reporting; collapsing them produces conclusions without a reproducible path. <Cite id="nist-sp800-86" locator="§3–4 (forensic process and evidence)" /> A screenshot can document a console view, but it is weaker than the source event, query and export that allow another analyst to reproduce the result.

| Ledger entry | Example | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirmed fact | Identity source records successful logon from device D-17 | Retain raw event, source clock and query |
| Interpretation | Credentials may be stolen | State alternatives: travel, new device, attacker |
| Missing evidence | Endpoint agent stopped reporting 25 minutes earlier | Open visibility-gap task; do not infer no process activity |
| Contradiction | Change ticket describes a test but names another laptop | Seek execution logs and device enrolment history |
| Action | Sessions revoked at 02:31 UTC by responder R-4 | Record approval, scope, outcome and rollback |

Order timeline entries by event time, but retain ingestion time beside them. When clocks differ, show the offset and the source rather than silently normalising to a precision the evidence does not support. Link each containment action to the evidence that triggered it and to the observed result. A session revocation at 02:31 and a failed sign-in at 02:33 support a containment claim; a disabled account alone does not prove that a stolen refresh token or persistence mechanism was removed.

## Data engineering

- **Schema:** adopt a common schema for users, hosts, IPs, processes and cloud resources.
- **Time:** synchronise clocks; record both event and ingestion time.
- **Retention:** hot searchable data versus cold archive; legal and privacy constraints.
- **Cost:** high-cardinality fields and full packet capture are expensive; measure value.
- **Reliability:** queueing, backpressure and parser failure handling.

## Automation and response

SOAR and automation help with enrichment, ticketing and repetitive containment. Automation needs:

1. explicit authorisation and guardrails,
2. idempotent actions,
3. audit logs,
4. rollback and human override,
5. tests for false positives.

<Callout variant="danger" title="Automated containment can automate an outage">
Isolating a host or disabling an account on a bad signal is an availability attack. Gate high-impact actions behind confidence thresholds and approval, and rehearse recovery.
</Callout>

## People and process

- Tiered triage works only with clear escalation criteria.
- Detection engineers and analysts should share ownership of rule quality.
- Run tabletop exercises for ransomware, identity compromise and cloud misconfiguration.
- Rotate on-call and measure workload; fatigue degrades judgement.
- Conduct blameless post-incident reviews and track action items.

### Deployment, scale and observability

Build the log pipeline as a queue with backpressure. Producers should retry with a bounded buffer, and collectors should expose ingestion lag by source. Partition hot data by time and source, but avoid exploding cardinality with user-controlled strings in index keys. A common schema reduces correlation cost; retain the original raw event in a protected tier when parsing fidelity matters. Define retention by investigative and legal value, not by available disk.

SIEM search cost depends on data volume, time range and concurrency. Keep high-value identity, endpoint and cloud events in hot storage; move raw payload captures and verbose debug logs to colder tiers. Measure search latency under concurrent investigations, not only ingestion throughput. Cache stable asset context and threat-intelligence lookups with freshness metadata. A fast enrichment service that returns confidently stale ownership is worse than a slower bounded lookup.

Use detection service-level indicators: source freshness, parser failure rate, rule execution errors, enrichment latency, case-creation rate and automation failure rate. Incident metrics should include time to detect, time to validate, time to contain and time to recover, each with a defined start and end event. Add quality measures: evidence completeness, containment success, rollback rate, reopened cases and false-negative findings from post-incident review. Never reward ticket closure without those companion measures.

### Testing and team operations

Every detection needs benign fixtures, malicious fixtures and boundary cases for late, duplicated and missing fields. Replay them through the production parser and query compiler. For correlation, test cross-source clock skew and identity changes. For response automation, test idempotency, partial failure and reversal. Run tabletop exercises with asset owners, legal, communications and executives so escalation is not invented during an outage.

Staffing follows case complexity, not alert count. Tier one validates and enriches; tier two investigates and hunts; incident command coordinates major events; detection engineers maintain content. Define handoffs with required evidence and escalation timers. Rotate on-call, protect focus time and track workload. Burnout causes missed signals and slow decisions, so it belongs in the operational risk register.

## Response trade-offs in a ransomware hour

At 03:12 UTC, a file server reports a spike in failed file opens. At 03:14, an endpoint alert reports a finance workstation disabling its protection agent and writing rapidly to a mapped share. Forty hosts share the subnet, one domain controller provides identity, and the last verified backup completed at 02:00. The SOC has enough evidence to suspect active ransomware but not yet enough to name the initial access path or prove that every affected host is known.

The first decision is scope of containment. Isolating only the reporting workstation may stop its writes but leave lateral movement to peers. Isolating the subnet can interrupt unrelated finance work but reduce spread. Disabling the compromised account may help, yet disabling the domain-controller computer account can break authentication for the whole organisation. The runbook should pre-authorise reversible, high-value actions and state which business owner must approve wider interruption. Malware incident guidance connects prevention and detection to containment, eradication and recovery rather than treating isolation as the end of response. <Cite id="nist-sp800-83r1" locator="§3–5" />

| Response option | Limits spread | Evidence cost | Service cost | Reversal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Block known malicious destination | Narrowly | Low | Usually low | Remove rule after scope review |
| Revoke user sessions and tokens | Across that identity | Low to moderate | Reauthentication required | Reissue after credential reset |
| Isolate reporting host | Stops that host's network access | Memory may be lost if action is destructive | High for that host | Reconnect after clean rebuild |
| Isolate subnet or segment | Broadly contains peers | May lose live remote evidence | High for all residents | Reconnect in controlled waves |
| Shut down file server | Stops shared writes | Volatile state may be lost | Critical outage | Restore from known-good backup |

If encryption is active, contain first and document what could not be preserved. If activity has stopped, acquire feasible volatile evidence before destructive action. In either branch, preserve identity, endpoint, file-audit and network logs outside the affected trust boundary. Record hashes after acquisition, tool versions, collector and timestamps; a hash establishes stability from the moment of hashing, not completeness of collection. <Cite id="nist-sp800-86" locator="§3–4 (forensic process and evidence)" />

Eradication then expands beyond deleting the malicious file: rotate affected credentials and tokens, remove persistence, rebuild compromised hosts from trusted images and verify that the entry path is closed. Recovery uses a known-good backup with a defined recovery point, restores service in controlled waves and monitors for reinfection. The incident-response lifecycle includes post-incident learning, so the final record must assign owners and deadlines for detection, architecture and recovery changes. <Cite id="sp800-61r3" locator="§2–5" />

Define timeline metrics with start and end events. Time to detect runs from the earliest supported malicious event to the first alert; time to validate from alert to a documented incident decision; time to contain from that decision to observed restriction of the threat; time to recover from containment initiation to verified service restoration. Pair each with evidence completeness, affected-asset coverage, rollback rate and recurrence. A fast containment that leaves persistence behind is not a fast recovery.

1. Inventory log sources with owner, schema, latency, reliability and retention.
2. Synchronise clocks and preserve event and ingestion timestamps.
3. Version detections, fixtures, parsers and runbooks together.
4. Measure precision, incident precision and analyst capacity by queue.
5. Group correlated alerts without discarding source evidence.
6. Gate automation by confidence, asset criticality and reversible outcome.
7. Protect evidence storage and record collection and transfer custody.
8. Exercise ransomware, identity compromise and cloud-control-plane scenarios.
9. Review missed detections and reopen causes after every major incident.
10. Balance speed metrics with evidence quality and containment effectiveness.

## Common failure modes

| Failure mode | Observable symptom | Root cause | Guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alert fatigue | Critical alerts wait behind scanner noise | Low precision and no capacity model | Tune, correlate and measure queue load |
| Silent telemetry loss | Detection appears quiet during an outage | No freshness or parser-failure alert | Source heartbeats and ingestion-lag monitoring |
| Rule drift | Previously reliable rule stops firing | Parser or schema changed | Versioned fixtures and production replay tests |
| Evidence loss | Investigation cannot reconstruct actions | Containment destroys state without capture | Evidence-first runbook with emergency exception |
| Automation outage | One bad signal isolates many hosts | No confidence gate or rate limit | Scoped, idempotent and reversible actions |
| Metric gaming | Fast closures with poor outcomes | Throughput rewarded without quality | Reopen, evidence and containment-success metrics |
| Unclear escalation | Major incident waits for approval | Roles and thresholds undefined | Incident-command matrix and tabletop drills |

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Correlation creates duplicate cases | Grouping key changes or window too broad | Stabilise identifiers and test window boundaries |
| Enrichment is slow or stale | Lookup service unavailable or cache expired | Add timeout, fallback and freshness indicators |
| Detection fires only in test | Production schema or clock differs | Compare parsed events and replay through production pipeline |
| Analyst cannot reproduce an alert | Query or raw event not retained | Store rule version, query and source evidence |
| Automated block affects a service | Asset context or confidence gate failed | Rate-limit, require approval and rehearse reversal |
| Post-incident actions never close | No owner, deadline or verification | Assign accountable owners and evidence-based closure criteria |

## Design review checklist

- [ ] Every telemetry source has an owner, schema, freshness target and failure alert.
- [ ] Event and ingestion time are preserved and clocks are synchronised.
- [ ] Detections state a testable hypothesis, required evidence and ATT&CK context.
- [ ] Precision and incident-level queue load are measured against analyst capacity.
- [ ] Runbooks define validation, escalation, containment, rollback and closure.
- [ ] Evidence storage protects integrity, custody, access and retention.
- [ ] Automation is scoped, idempotent, audited and reversible.
- [ ] Critical-asset actions have an approval path and business fallback.
- [ ] Tabletop exercises include legal, communications and asset owners.
- [ ] Metrics pair speed with evidence quality, reopen rate and missed detections.

## Key takeaways

- A SOC turns telemetry into defensible decisions; alerts are hypotheses, not incidents.

- Detection engineering is product engineering: owners, tests, metrics and retirement.
- Base rates and response cost determine whether a detection is useful.
- Evidence preservation and escalation are part of response, not afterthoughts.
- Automation needs guardrails; a wrong block is an outage.
