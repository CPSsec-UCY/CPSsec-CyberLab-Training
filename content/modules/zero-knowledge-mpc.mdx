## Two related promises

A **zero-knowledge proof** convinces a verifier that a statement is true without revealing the secret witness. “I know the password” can become “I can prove I know the password” without sending it.

**Secure multi-party computation (MPC)** lets parties compute a function of private inputs while revealing only the result. Hospitals can compute a shared statistic without exchanging patient records; banks can screen for fraud without pooling customers.

## What neither tool does

- A ZK proof does not make the underlying data true. It proves a relation about committed values.
- MPC does not fix bad inputs, collusion or compromised endpoints. It protects inputs under a stated adversary model.

<Callout variant="note" title="The verifier learns the statement, not the witness">
A zero-knowledge proof reveals the fact being proved and usually the protocol transcript. It hides the witness and anything else the proof system promises to hide.
</Callout>

## Why it matters

Privacy-preserving credentials, confidential transactions, private auctions, threshold custody and cross-organisation analytics all depend on these ideas. The cost is usually performance and complexity, not a lack of theory.

## A concrete privacy problem

A consortium of banks wants to detect fraud patterns across customers without pooling transaction records. MPC can compute a joint statistic or train a model over encrypted inputs, revealing only the agreed output. A zero-knowledge proof can let one bank prove that its contribution satisfies a policy — for example, that no customer exceeds a reporting threshold — without revealing the underlying accounts.

The hard part is not the primitive. It is defining the policy, deciding what the output itself reveals, and proving that the deployed circuit implements the policy. A private computation that outputs a per-customer score can still violate privacy.

## Threat model: who learns what

Consider a bank proving eligibility for a syndicated loan. The public statement is that its exposure is below a threshold; the witness contains the account balances and identity mappings used to calculate that exposure. A verifier should learn the eligibility result, not the balances. In the companion MPC computation, five banks may learn the total exposure, but no bank should learn another bank's individual contribution. These are different disclosure rules, so they need separate specifications.

The attacker may eavesdrop on messages, submit chosen public statements, corrupt endpoints, or control one participant in the computation. A stronger MPC attacker controls two colluding participants and deliberately sends malformed messages. The defender must preserve input privacy, authenticate the intended public key, detect invalid statements, and ensure that one participant cannot force the others to reveal shares during recovery. If fairness is required, either all parties receive the output or none do; if only security against abort is promised, an adversary may prevent delivery while learning nothing.

Write the model before choosing a proof system. Record the number of malicious parties $t$, whether corruption is static or adaptive, whether messages can be delayed or reordered, and which parties see the result. A proof verifier is usually a passive machine, but a malicious verifier can send challenges adaptively and retain transcripts. A protocol that is secure against an eavesdropper is not automatically secure against that verifier.

The defender's goals also include bounded work and verifiable parameter provenance. A prover should not be tricked into proving a different predicate through a reused challenge, and an MPC participant should not accept a share vector that reconstructs inconsistently. Availability is separate: an adversary can always refuse to participate even when privacy and correctness hold.

Common misconceptions make the model smaller than reality. Zero knowledge does not mean the proof has no metadata; proof length, proving time and repeated statements can leak application information. MPC does not make colluding parties honest, and a threshold does not protect an output that deliberately identifies every input. Finally, privacy of intermediate values and privacy of the final answer are distinct claims. A sum can be protected during computation yet disclose a sole nonzero contributor when released.

### Start with a relation and a disclosure map

Let $R \subseteq X \times W$ be a relation. A statement $x\in X$ is true when there exists a witness $w\in W$ such that $(x,w)\in R$. The language $L_R$ is the set of such statements. A proof system must specify the algorithms, the security parameter and what public auxiliary information is available. “Prove the balance is below the limit” is incomplete until the balance's commitment, ownership relation, allowed range, bank identity and loan cycle are part of $x$ or the auxiliary input.

In the formal distinction, a **proof** has information-theoretic soundness, while an **argument** may rely on computational soundness against efficient adversaries. A SNARK is an argument of knowledge: an efficient convincing prover is tied to a witness an extractor could recover. “Negligible” is relative to the security parameter. Zero knowledge must hide the witness from the verifier's entire view, including public inputs, messages, timing and auxiliary information.

Create a disclosure map before selecting a primitive. Mark each value as public, witness-only, threshold-shared, verifier-visible or output, including intermediates and metadata. A proof may hide balances while revealing the bank; MPC may hide contributions while revealing a total; threshold signing may hide the key while revealing participants. These claims need different protocols.

The adversary card should name corrupted parties, corruption timing, semi-honest or malicious behaviour, and network control. Can a verifier choose statements adaptively or retain transcripts? Can an MPC party send inconsistent inputs, abort, correlate sessions or learn the output alone? Fairness, security against abort and guaranteed delivery are separate requirements.

Finally, cryptographic validity is not real-world truth. A proof can certify arithmetic over wrong source data, and MPC can correctly compute over poisoned inputs. The application still needs authenticated ingestion, policy ownership, disputes and output-release decisions. Privacy-preserving computation narrows who must be trusted; it does not remove trust in input meaning or policy correctness.

## Interactive proof in one example

For a public group with generator $g$ and public key $y = g^x$:

1. Prover samples $r$ and sends $t = g^r$.
2. Verifier sends a random challenge $c$.
3. Prover sends $s = r + c x$.
4. Verifier checks $g^s = t y^c$.

The verifier learns that the prover knows $x$, not $x$ itself. Hashing the challenge with the statement (Fiat–Shamir) makes the proof non-interactive. <Cite id="fiat-shamir1986" locator="pp. 186–194" /> <Cite id="gmr1989" locator="Vol. 18, pp. 186–208" />

## Proof systems

- **SNARKs** provide small proofs and fast verification, often with a trusted setup. Groth16 is a widely used pairing-based construction. <Cite id="groth2016" locator="pp. 305–326" />
- **STARKs** avoid a trusted setup and rely on hash-based assumptions, at the cost of larger proofs. <Cite id="stark2018" locator="Abstract and §1–2" />
- **Bulletproofs** provide short range proofs without a trusted setup. <Cite id="bulletproofs2018" locator="pp. 315–334" />
- **Sigma protocols** generalise the Schnorr pattern for many relations.

## MPC building blocks

- **Secret sharing:** split a secret into shares; a threshold reconstructs it, fewer shares reveal nothing. Shamir secret sharing is the canonical construction. <Cite id="shamir1979" locator="Vol. 22, pp. 612–613" />

## Threat model first

Ask who may be malicious, how many parties may collude, whether the network is synchronous, and whether fairness or output privacy is required. A semi-honest protocol assumes parties follow the protocol; a malicious model must also handle incorrect inputs and selective abort.



## The proof and MPC stack

A zero-knowledge proof system has a prover, a verifier and a statement. The prover knows a witness; the verifier checks a proof. SNARKs make proofs small and verification cheap, often with a trusted setup and pairing-based assumptions. STARKs avoid the setup but produce larger proofs. <Cite id="stark2018" locator="Abstract and §1–2" /> Bulletproofs are useful for range and confidential-value proofs without a trusted setup. <Cite id="groth2016" locator="pp. 305–326" /> <Cite id="bulletproofs2018" locator="pp. 315–334" />

MPC has complementary building blocks: secret sharing distributes a value, garbled circuits evaluate a Boolean circuit between two parties, and secret-sharing protocols evaluate arithmetic or Boolean circuits among many parties. Oblivious transfer and commitments supply the glue. <Cite id="yao1986" locator="pp. 162–167" /> <Cite id="shamir1979" locator="Vol. 22, pp. 612–613" />

## What the proof does not establish

A proof establishes that a witness satisfies the circuit. It does not establish that the witness corresponds to real-world facts, that the input was not manipulated, or that the output is safe to reveal. Those are application and governance questions.

## From a relation to a protocol

A relation is a set of pairs $(x,w)$: the public statement $x$ and a witness $w$. Before writing a circuit, express it as constraints. For the loan example, $x$ might be a root committing to a balance sheet, and $w$ contains opening values and ownership proofs. The circuit checks that the same values feed both the commitment and the exposure sum. Merely checking that a number is below a threshold proves nothing about the account from which it came.

For Schnorr, choose a group of known order $p$, a generator $g$, secret exponent $x$, and public key $y=g^x$. The prover samples a nonce $r$ uniformly, sends $t=g^r$, receives a challenge $c$, and responds with $s=r+cx \bmod p$. Verification recomputes $g^s$ and $t y^c$. If either side uses different bytes for $y$ or $c$, verification fails; if the prover reuses $r$ with different challenges, two responses expose a linear equation about $x$.

Shamir sharing chooses a random polynomial $f$ of degree at most $t$ with constant term equal to the secret. Party $i$ receives $f(i)$. Any $t$ points determine no information about $f(0)$ beyond what was already known; any $t+1$ points interpolate it. For multiplication, additive shares are useful because local addition is free. Parties use preprocessed multiplication triples so that neither the inputs nor the product are opened during the protocol.

For MPC, translate the computation into a fixed sequence of additions and multiplications. Check that every intermediate has an owner, a sharing representation and a reconstruction point. ### Fiat–Shamir is a protocol specification, not a hash call

For Schnorr, the interactive transcript is $(t,c,s)$. Fiat–Shamir replaces the verifier's challenge with a hash of a canonical byte string, for example

$$
c = H(\text{domain}\Vert\text{version}\Vert x\Vert\text{aux}\Vert t).
$$

Every public input that affects the relation must be bound. The domain tag separates authentication, credential and transaction uses; the version prevents silent interpretation changes; auxiliary input captures verifier-specific policy; and $t$ commits to the prover's first move. The encoding must be unambiguous, and the hash output must be mapped into the declared challenge set without bias. A verifier should reject non-canonical group elements and responses outside the scalar range before doing expensive checks.

The random-oracle model is part of the security argument. In the reduction, the simulator programs hash responses and can sometimes answer a challenge it chose after seeing $t$, which an honest interactive verifier would not allow. The reduction guesses which programmed query corresponds to the eventual forgery and loses a factor in success probability. Challenge length, domain separation and a correct hash-to-field or hash-to-group encoding are therefore security parameters, not formatting choices. Replay protection is separate: a proof for a valid statement may remain valid forever unless the statement includes a nonce, expiry or state root that the verifier checks.

Nonce freshness is equally important. If the prover reuses $r$, both proofs have the same commitment $t$. Subtracting their responses gives $s_1-s_2=(c_1-c_2)x$ in the exponent group. When $c_1-c_2$ is invertible, the secret exponent is exposed. This failure does not require breaking the hash or solving a discrete logarithm; it comes from reusing randomness. A prover must use an approved random source, bind randomness to the session, and survive crashes or rollback without repeating a nonce.

### A concrete loan-proof circuit

Suppose the public statement contains a domain tag, loan-cycle identifier, bank identifier, committed balance-sheet root and internal exposure cap. The witness contains individual balances and commitment openings. The circuit first verifies each opening against the committed leaf, then constrains the sum of those same balances to equal the bank's exposure. A range-checked bit decomposition proves the exposure is non-negative and does not overflow; a comparison constraint proves it is below the cap. Finally, the bank identifier and cycle are copied into the public-input vector and hashed into the Fiat–Shamir challenge.

Each constraint prevents a specific false proof. Checking only the sum permits an invented exposure; checking the sum without the commitment permits unrelated balances; omitting range and overflow checks can let an out-of-range signed value satisfy an unsigned comparison. Omitting the bank identifier permits replay under another bank's name. The verifier checks the relation, canonical public inputs, parameter hashes and application state without learning the balances.

For the companion MPC computation, represent the same values as shares and evaluate a fixed circuit. Comparison and selection must operate on shared values or a proved branch condition; letting one bank choose a private branch can leak information. Declare the output set—total, threshold decision or per-bank receipt—before compiling, because circuit design cannot repair an unsafe disclosure requirement.

## The three properties

- **Completeness:** an honest prover with a valid witness convinces the verifier.
- **Soundness:** a prover without a witness cannot convince the verifier except with small probability.
- **Zero knowledge:** a simulator can produce transcripts indistinguishable from real ones without the witness.

<Callout variant="lab" title="Simulation is the definition">
For Schnorr, the simulator picks $c$ and $s$ first, then sets $t = g^s y^{-c}$. The resulting transcript is distributed like a real proof, so the proof leaks nothing beyond validity. The random-oracle model is doing real work when Fiat–Shamir replaces the interactive challenge.
</Callout>

## Trusted setup

Some SNARKs require a structured reference string. A compromised ceremony can enable false proofs. Multi-party ceremonies reduce the risk, but you must verify the parameters and the ceremony transcript. Transparent proof systems avoid this trust assumption at a performance cost.

## MPC security

For secret-sharing MPC, correctness follows from linearity of sharing and a secure multiplication protocol. Security is argued by simulation: whatever the adversary sees can be generated from its inputs and outputs. The number of corruptions, corruption timing and network model all matter.

## Output leakage

Even a perfect MPC reveals the output. If the function's output identifies an individual or allows differencing attacks, the protocol is not enough. Combine MPC with differential privacy or query restrictions when the output itself is sensitive.



## Completeness, soundness and simulation

- **Completeness:** an honest prover with a valid witness convinces the verifier.
- **Soundness:** a prover without a witness cannot convince the verifier except with small probability.
- **Zero knowledge:** a simulator can generate transcripts indistinguishable from real ones without the witness.

For Schnorr, the simulator chooses the challenge $c$ and response $s$ first, then sets $t = g^s y^{-c}$. In the random-oracle model, Fiat–Shamir turns the interactive protocol into a non-interactive one; domain separation and challenge binding are essential.

## Trusted setup

A SNARK setup can produce a structured reference string. If the toxic waste is retained, false proofs may be possible. Multi-party ceremonies reduce the risk, but the verifier must authenticate the parameters and the ceremony transcript. Transparent systems avoid this trust assumption at a performance and proof-size cost.

## MPC security models

A semi-honest protocol assumes parties follow the protocol but inspect the transcript. A malicious model must also handle incorrect inputs, selective abort and collusion. Security is stated relative to a corruption threshold and a network model (synchronous or asynchronous). The output itself may leak; MPC alone does not provide differential privacy.

## Precise properties and reductions

For a relation $R$, completeness says that for every $(x,w)\in R$, an honest prover using fresh randomness makes an honest verifier accept except with a probability explicitly allowed by the definition. Soundness says that for every $x\notin L_R$, every efficient prover, even one that chooses its strategy after seeing $x$, has only negligible probability of producing an accepted proof. Knowledge soundness is stronger: an extractor can recover a witness from a convincing prover under the stated setup and random-oracle assumptions.

Zero knowledge compares two distributions of entire verifier views. An ideal functionality gives the simulator the statement and allowed public auxiliary information but not the witness. If a simulator can reproduce the real view, then the real interaction adds no usable witness information. This must hold against a verifier that chooses challenges adaptively, not merely against a fixed challenge sequence.

Schnorr's interactive zero-knowledge simulator illustrates the quantifiers. It chooses $c$ and $s$ uniformly, sets $t=g^s y^{-c}$, and programs the random-oracle challenge at $(x,t)$ to $c$. The simulated transcript satisfies verification. Without the random-oracle programming step, choosing $c$ after seeing $t$ does not model a real verifier that commits its challenge first. Special soundness gives an extractor: two accepting transcripts with the same $t$ but challenges $c_1\ne c_2$ yield $s_1-s_2=(c_1-c_2)x \bmod p$, so the extractor solves for $x$ when the challenge difference is invertible.

Fiat–Shamir replaces the interactive challenge with $H(x\|t)$. The reduction rewrites a non-interactive forger into an interactive adversary by programming the hash and guessing which programmed challenge the forgery will use. That guess causes a loss in the reduction. Domain separation prevents cross-protocol reuse. Challenge length must make guessing negligible, and the hash encoding must map unambiguously to a group element or field value. A verifier must still reject a response outside the canonical field or scalar range.

For $n$ parties with a static threshold $t$, a secret-sharing MPC protocol can provide perfect input privacy when $n>2t$ and the corruption view is simulated using the corrupt parties' own inputs and outputs. Correctness follows inductively: addition preserves shares, the multiplication protocol reconstructs the product correctly, and reconstruction reveals only the declared function output. In the Beaver example, the local output shares sum to the true product because $xy=ab+b(x-a)+a(y-b)+(x-a)(y-b)$.

The security argument must also cover malicious behaviour. Semi-honest security does not let the simulator invent inconsistent inputs for a corrupt party; that is precisely what a malicious adversary may do. Authenticated shares, input-consistency checks, commitments, or zero-knowledge proofs of correct local computation address this gap. Selective abort requires an additional argument that the adversary learns nothing from the shares it saw before refusing to continue. Protocol composition is not automatic: an adversary controlling parties in two invoked subprotocols may correlate their random tapes unless independence is specified.

Edge cases belong in the statement. What happens when the witness is empty, the public input is duplicated, the verifier reuses randomness, or the same proof is replayed? Replay protection may require a nonce or state outside the proof. What happens when MPC output is undefined, such as division by zero? The function must define an abort or shared sentinel. None of these cases is fixed by calling the system zero knowledge or secure.

### Quantifiers, extraction and corruption thresholds

Completeness should state an error bound: for every valid pair $(x,w)$, an honest prover with fresh randomness makes an honest verifier accept with probability at least $1-\alpha(\lambda)$; perfect completeness sets the error to zero. Soundness quantifies over all false statements and efficient provers, including statements chosen after seeing public parameters. Knowledge soundness adds an extractor that recovers a witness whenever a convincing prover succeeds, under the stated assumptions. <Cite id="groth2016" locator="§2.2" />

Zero knowledge also has gradations. Perfect zero knowledge means the simulated and real transcript distributions are identical; statistical zero knowledge allows a negligible statistical distance; computational zero knowledge allows efficient distinguishers only a negligible advantage. In every case the simulator receives the statement and permitted auxiliary information but not the witness. The definition must cover a malicious verifier that chooses challenges adaptively and may interact with many sessions. <Cite id="gmr1989" locator="Vol. 18, pp. 186–208" />

MPC uses a related ideal–real argument. The real adversary controls the declared corrupt parties and sees their inputs, messages and outputs. A simulator must reproduce that view using only the same corrupt inputs and the ideal function's output. Privacy follows when the view can be simulated; correctness follows when honest parties' outputs match the function. Malicious security additionally requires input consistency, authenticated shares and safe handling of selective abort. A protocol secure against semi-honest parties does not automatically meet that standard.

Thresholds are protocol-specific. In a static semi-honest secret-sharing model, perfect input privacy commonly requires an honest majority, $n>2t$, where $t$ is the corruption bound. Active malicious security can require a stricter bound and extra mechanisms; asynchronous variants may be stricter again. Never transfer a threshold between protocols or network models. <Cite id="ben-or-goldwasser-wigderson1988" locator="pp. 1–10" /> Five banks with one permitted corruption and a three-of-five reconstruction threshold are safe only if the chosen protocol&apos;s assumptions support it; the arithmetic alone does not prove malicious security.

## Engineering costs

- **Circuit size** dominates proof time and memory; a cleaner circuit can save orders of magnitude.
- **Prover cost** is much higher than verifier cost; mobile provers are difficult.
- **Trusted setup** adds ceremony, parameter distribution and verification burden.
- **MPC networking** dominates WAN deployments; latency and bandwidth shape protocol choice.
- **Threshold signing** avoids reconstructing a key but introduces coordination and abort handling.

## Side channels and randomness

Proofs and MPC protocols can leak through timing, memory access, power and abort behaviour. Randomness is part of the security boundary: a biased nonce can reveal a witness. Protect proving keys and shares as crown jewels.

<Callout variant="danger" title="Do not accept “zero-knowledge” as a security proof">
Ask what is hidden, from whom, under what collusion and network assumptions, and what the output itself reveals. A proof system with a broken setup, a leaky prover or a revealing output can fail operationally.
</Callout>

## Implementation patterns and operational review

Keep circuit generation deterministic and versioned with the policy. A witness generator should consume typed, validated records and fail closed before proving. Separate public inputs from witness memory so that a logging wrapper cannot accidentally serialise the witness. For a SNARK, pin the proving-key hash, verifier-key hash, compiler version and setup transcript; a valid proof under the wrong key is still a deployment failure.

Treat the prover as a high-value signing process. Give it a dedicated worker, bounded queues and controlled concurrency. Use an approved random source for every nonce or witness commitment. Zeroise witness buffers on success and error, and prevent core dumps from copying them. If the prover is remote, authenticate the request and return a proof bound to the exact public-input digest the caller intended.

For MPC, schedule rounds rather than passing an unrestricted message stream between peers. Each message needs a protocol identifier, session identifier, party identity, round number and authenticated payload. Buffer incomplete rounds, reject inconsistent duplicate values and define timeouts that do not reconstruct secrets. A participant that disconnects may cause abort; it must not be able to request extra shares simply by claiming to be the recovery coordinator.

Measure constraint count, proving time, peak memory and proof bytes separately. A circuit with one million constraints and one with two million may differ by more than a factor of two once preprocessing is included. For MPC, record round latency, bytes per party and recovery time on the actual WAN. Avoid quoting a localhost benchmark as a production latency.

Test three layers. First, unit tests compare tiny circuits against the same predicate evaluated on ordinary integers. Second, adversarial tests mutate every public input, proof element and share message, including non-canonical encodings and truncated arrays. Third, end-to-end tests restore a prover or MPC participant from backup while another request is active. Include a setup-parameter mismatch and a compromised random source in the test plan; both should produce a clear refusal rather than a plausible-looking result.

During operation, monitor proof acceptance rates, proving latency, participant dropout and output-query volume. A sudden rise in proof failures can mean a compiler upgrade changed the circuit encoding. Repeated tiny outputs from an MPC service can reveal a changed dataset through differencing. Rate limits and a documented release schedule are therefore security controls, not only abuse controls.

When a proof fails, first verify the exact public inputs and parameter hashes, then reproduce with the known-good compiler. Do not regenerate setup parameters as a troubleshooting shortcut. When an MPC round fails, preserve authenticated logs of public metadata, identify the last committed round, and restart from a defined checkpoint without broadcasting shares. Verify-before-release is useful for signatures produced by MPC, but it does not replace checking that all required parties approved the same message.

### Parameter lifecycle and leakage beyond the mathematics

In Groth's formulation, setup returns a common reference string and a simulation trapdoor; possessing the trapdoor changes the security claim. <Cite id="groth2016" locator="§2.2" /> A multi-party ceremony is useful only if contributions are independently generated, authenticated and verifiable, and at least one participant destroys its secret. Record the circuit hash, compiler version, contributors, contribution hashes and final parameter hashes. Reject parameters from an unverified build even when individual proofs pass local tests.

Treat the prover as a high-value process with a leakage surface larger than its algebra. Timing and memory-access patterns can depend on secret branches; power and cache behaviour can expose operations; faults or aborts can reveal which check failed. Kocher's timing work and differential-power analysis demonstrate why constant-time arithmetic alone does not protect the whole witness pipeline. <Cite id="kocher1996" locator="pp. 104–113" /> <Cite id="kocher1999" locator="pp. 388–397" /> Isolate proving workers, bound concurrency, zeroise witness buffers, disable core dumps and test error paths as carefully as successful proofs.

MPC needs equivalent lifecycle controls for shares and preprocessing data. Authenticate every session and round, reject stale or inconsistent shares, regenerate compromised multiplication triples and never reconstruct merely because a participant claims to coordinate recovery. A malicious party may always abort; if fairness is required, the protocol needs a delivery or recovery mechanism rather than a promise that the network will behave.

Finally, govern the output independently of the circuit. Define retention, authorised recipients, query budgets and suppression rules for small cohorts or differencing releases. A perfect proof and private computation still produce a public answer. Parameter compromise, witness exposure and unsafe output release require different incident owners, evidence and remediation; combining them into one “crypto incident” category hides the trust assumptions that actually failed.

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Why it happens | Consequence | Prevention |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Witness appears in logs | A generic request serializer includes the proof input object | Permanent secret disclosure | Use separate public and private request types |
| Proof replay across statements | Challenge omits a public input or domain tag | Acceptance of a proof for the wrong claim | Canonicalise and hash every public input |
| Setup parameters are unverified | Proving and verifier keys come from different builds | False rejection or unsound deployment | Pin hashes and verify the ceremony transcript |
| Circuit disagrees with policy | Constraints implement a simplified predicate | Valid proof of a false business statement | Differential tests against policy examples |
| One party learns a full secret during MPC recovery | Recovery requests shares without checking the threshold | Defeat of input privacy | Authenticate participants and reconstruct only at a quorum |
| Output reveals a contributor | A protected computation releases a uniquely identifying result | Inference despite secure evaluation | Apply cohort, differencing and release controls |
| Randomness is reused | Snapshot rollback or a faulty nonce service repeats coins | Secret recovery or broken simulation | Monotonic nonce policy and approved randomness |
| Malicious party can abort freely | Protocol promises fairness but has no recovery or output commitment | Denial of service | Specify the abort model and add a recovery design |

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Every proof fails after a compiler upgrade | Constraint ordering or public-input encoding changed | Reproduce with the pinned compiler and compare the public-input byte string |
| Verification succeeds locally but fails in production | Proving and verifier parameter hashes differ | Distribute parameters as an authenticated bundle and verify both hashes |
| MPC parties reconstruct different values | Inconsistent shares or a corrupted multiplication triple | Abort the session, check share commitments and regenerate preprocessing data |
| A restored party rejoins an old session | Session identifiers or round state were not checkpointed | Bind recovery to a fresh session and reject stale rounds |
| Proving memory grows under load | Concurrent witnesses and un-cleared worker buffers | Limit concurrency, reuse bounded workspaces and zeroise after each request |
| Daily totals expose a small bank | Output policy ignores differencing and small cohorts | Enforce suppression and query budgets before release |

## Design review checklist

- [ ] The statement, witness, public inputs and auxiliary information are written precisely.
- [ ] The adversary, corruption threshold, network model and abort guarantee are explicit.
- [ ] The challenge binds the protocol domain, version, statement and commitment.
- [ ] Circuit constraints are tested against independent policy examples and boundary values.
- [ ] Setup parameters and compiler artifacts are authenticated and pinned by hash.
- [ ] Witnesses and MPC shares are excluded from logs, crash dumps and telemetry.
- [ ] Randomness generation, nonce uniqueness and backup behaviour are tested.
- [ ] Malformed proofs, non-canonical encodings and inconsistent shares fail closed.
- [ ] MPC recovery reconstructs only from an authorised quorum and never broadcasts shares.
- [ ] Output privacy, differencing risk and release quotas have an independent review.
- [ ] Proving memory, MPC round latency and worst-case queueing are measured under load.

## Key takeaways

- ZK proofs prove statements about witnesses; MPC computes over private inputs.
- Completeness, soundness and zero knowledge are the core proof properties.
- SNARKs trade setup and assumptions for small proofs; transparent systems trade size for trust.
- MPC protects inputs under a stated corruption and network model.
- Circuit correctness, randomness and output privacy are part of the security boundary.
